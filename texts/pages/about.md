## About this map

This interactive map is for practising the geography of the Japanese Empire: the home islands, the formal colonies, the occupied territories and puppet states, the neighbouring countries, and the cities, ports and battlefields that recur in the history of modern Japan from Perry's arrival in 1853 to the surrender in 1945.

### How to use it

* **1930 / Dec 1942** — which map you are looking at. In 1930 the colours show whose empire each place belonged to, on the eve of the Manchurian Incident. In December 1942 they show how Japan held what it had taken.
* **Explore** — hover over, or tap, any territory or marker to see what it is, what it was called, when it changed hands, and why it matters.
* **Quiz** — you are asked to find a place and you click it on the map. Your score and the places you missed are reported at the end.
* **Basic / Intermediate / Advanced** — how many places the quiz asks about, and how many names appear on the map. Every territory stays clickable at every level.
* **Names in four scripts** — every script a place answers to, shown at once rather than chosen between. The headline is English, giving the name as it was used at the time with the present-day one after it where they differ: Mukden (Shenyang), Taihoku (Taipei), Peking (Beijing), Batavia (Jakarta). Under it come the others, and only those that belong to the place: Japanese in the imperial-period form — Keijō for Seoul, Shinkyō for Changchun, Shōnantō for Singapore — Chinese in traditional characters, as the period used them, and Korean in hangul with McCune-Reischauer romanisation. Explanatory notes stay in English throughout, as do the names of the provinces of Siam and the Philippines, which is how the period's own maps of them are lettered.

### A caution about borders

Inside China the boundaries are the real provinces of 1928–45, so Manchukuo is Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang and Jehol as they actually stood, and Mengchiang is Chahar and Suiyuan. Karafuto is Sakhalin south of the 50th parallel, which is where the border ran, and the Kwantung Leased Territory is cut across the Liaodong isthmus on the line of the 1898 lease. Elsewhere, modern coastlines and outlines stand in for historical ones — fine for coasts, rough for land borders.

The area of occupied China is traced from a period map of the occupation rather than drawn as whole provinces, and adjusted to December 1942: the front runs down the Tatung–Puchow railway in Shansi to the Yellow River bend, along the river's north bank — so Loyang and Chengchow are outside it, both holding until 1944 — then down the flood course the river took after Huayuankou, round the Chinese pocket in north-west Anhui and the Dabie Shan, out to Ichang, past Yochow, and through Chekiang taking in Kinhwa but not Chuchow or Wenchow. It is still approximate, and it is still generous: Japanese control ran along the railways and around the cities, and much of the countryside inside the line was held by Communist and Nationalist guerrillas.

Hovering names the sub-unit under the pointer as well as the country: the province, prefecture, residency, state, division or island. These are the units of the period and they carry their period names — Kankyōhoku-dō rather than North Hamgyŏng, Tayabas rather than Quezon, Chiengmai rather than Chiang Mai, Celebes rather than Sulawesi. Their outlines, though, are assembled from modern ones — Korea excepted — so where a boundary has moved since, the shape is an approximation and the name is the thing to trust. Three places to keep in mind. Manchukuo redrew the three north-eastern provinces into nineteen of its own between 1934 and 1941; they are drawn here as Fengtien, Kirin and Heilungkiang, the provinces Manchukuo was made out of. British India's provinces were interleaved with several hundred princely states, which are drawn from a layer of their 1931 boundaries rather than approximated from modern ones; only the smallest are below the resolution of this map, and those are drawn inside whichever province surrounded them. And Siam's changwat sat inside the monthon, the "circles" abolished in 1933, which are not drawn. Korea is the exception to all of this: its thirteen provinces come from a map of the period rather than from modern units, so Hwanghae is one province and Kaesong is in Keiki-dō, where it belonged.

Where several modern states made up one historical territory they are drawn as a single shape with no boundary between them, because there was none: British India has no line at the Punjab, French Indochina none at the Mekong, Korea none at the 38th parallel. Present-day borders that had not been drawn yet do not appear.

The front line moved constantly between 1931 and 1945, and any single date is a snapshot. For close work, use a proper historical atlas.

### Sources and credits

**[Every dataset this map is drawn from is listed here](sources.html)**, with what each was used for, where it came from and under what licence, and a note on what was done to the data. In short: coastlines from Natural Earth, China's provinces the real ones of 1928–45, Korea from a map of the period, the princely states georeferenced from a 1931 atlas, and the occupied zone and the line of control traced from period maps and approximate. The fine coastlines and island names of the Ryukyus, the Bonins, Micronesia and the Solomons are ©&nbsp;OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL, and load only when you zoom in on them.

The place list is drawn from the timelines of modern Japanese history used in this module, which in turn rest on Andrew Gordon, *A Modern History of Japan*; Elise K. Tipton, *Modern Japan*; Brett L. Walker, *A Concise History of Japan*; Mikiso Hane and Louise G. Perez, *Modern Japan*; Kenneth Henshall, *Historical Dictionary of Japan to 1945*; and Jeremy A. Yellen, *Japan at War, 1914–1952*.
