### How to use it

* **1930 / Dec 1942** — which map you are looking at. In 1930 the colours show whose empire each place belonged to, on the eve of the Manchurian Incident. In December 1942 they show how Japan held what it had taken. You may may also choose to view the general approximate extent of Japanese occupation in China (which hides the fragmented nature of that occupation), or switch to a layer which shows a version of a September, 1942 Japanese military report's depiction of so-called "pacified" and "un-pacified" areas.
* **Explore** — hover over, or tap, any territory or marker to see what it is, what it was called, when it changed hands, and why it matters. Many entries also provide links to Wikipedia entries when these are available.
* **Pinning an outline** — every line this map draws round a shape answers the question *where is the pointer now*, so it goes the moment you pan or zoom away. **Cmd-click** (**Ctrl-click** on Windows) to keep one: the shape is outlined in thick bright yellow, and that outline stays put through any amount of panning and zooming, which is what you want when the point of the exercise is *here is Shandong — now look at the rest of the country*. With **Admin** off it holds the country; with **Admin** on it holds the division under the pointer. Click anywhere inside it to take it off again.

* ** Cities, Events, Administrative boundaries, and other labels** - Buttons at the top of the screen allow you to toggle the visibility of cities, some historical events, administrative boundaries for some of the countries shown and other labels. These may be the same in the 1930 and 1942 maps or may differ somewhat.
* **Which names** - **Other** writes five kinds of name at once: countries and colonies, cities, provinces, places of interest, and seas, mountains and other natural features. They can be had separately. Hold the **Other** button down for half a second — or option-click it — for a short menu of the five, and the same five rows are under **Labels** in the Layers pane. Ticking one with Other off turns Other on; unticking the last one turns it off again.
* **The key as a set of switches** - Hold down the year at the top of the key for half a second, or option-click it, and every row that stands for something on the map grows a tick box. Untick **Colonies & leased territory** and the colonies come off — or press the arrow beside it to open the six out and untick Kwantung alone, leaving Chōsen and Taiwan on. Rows that already have a switch elsewhere — Cities, Events, Places of interest, the rivers, the line of control — are that switch, so the Layers pane follows along. **Reset map** appears under the list once something has been taken off.
* **Other Layers** - See the Layers pane for options to add or swap out layers to display.
* **Island Details** - Some islands will get swapped out with a more detailed one as you zoom in closer to them. 
* **Train Tools** - Open **Layers** and tick **Train Tools** under Transport, then zoom in to a railway that has a timetable — Taiwan for now. The lines are drawn in the colours the timetable gives them, and a bar at the foot of the map runs the day: press play and the trains move at the rate you choose, from two minutes a second to twenty. Tap a station for the trains that called there, a moving train for what it is and where it is going, or the track itself for the line and a day's working on it — each with a link to its own printed table. On a large screen that table opens over the map rather than in another tab. It reads in English, Chinese or Japanese at the press of a button, and gives each station's reading under its name. Station and line names in the cards follow **Use Japanese names**: Mandarin with the characters after it by default, the Japanese reading when that is ticked. The timetable is February 1936, which is neither of this map's two dates. Zooming back out puts the tools away, and so does the train button beside the zoom controls; the railway stays, drawn plainly in one colour. The button above it marks the stations on any railway you have switched on.
* **Demography** - Open **Layers** and choose one of the maps under **Demography** — Korea has three: population density, citizenship, occupation — or press **Provinces by Population Density** at the foot of any Korean province's card — they are the same switch. The thirteen provinces are shaded by people per square kilometre in five classes, with the figure itself written on each one — dark on the pale bands, white on the deep. Taiwan's fifty-five districts are numbered the same way, the larger ones from the island view and the rest as you zoom in far enough to letter them. A unit the source has no figure for is left white and says so. Which year is drawn follows the map you are on, and the key at the left names it, says where each class begins and gives the source. Point at a province — or, on a phone, tap the country and then the province — for its population, the sex ratio, its share of Korea and its density; the same figures are in the card. Cheju carries Zenranan-dō's, because the 1942 returns count it there and do not break the island out. A Taiwanese 郡 or 市 carries the resident population of its date — the household registers at the end of 1930 on one map and of 1941 on the other — the prefecture it belonged to, and how many of its people were counted as Japanese, Taiwanese, Korean, Chinese or other foreign, and a Taiwanese city dot carries the 市 itself — seven of them in 1930 and eleven in 1941, four of the eleven raised to 市 after the boundaries this map draws, so the shape under those dots is still the district with the city inside it and the card says so. The two dates count the Government-General's demarcated "Aborigine Territory" differently and each card says which: in 1930 the Indigenous Peoples — 「蕃人」 in the source's own word — are counted there and nowhere else, so the fifty-five districts and that territory divide the colony between them exactly; in 1941 the same people are counted again in the district their ground lies in. The territory itself is left white on both, the source giving it no area to divide by. On the 1930 map the card also carries what the Korean census counted: the ages, the household registers and nationalities, and the nine categories of occupation — for the colony, for each province, and for each of the fourteen 府. **Population Table** under the figures opens the whole column — the colony, then the provinces by size — with the source at the foot. It is one wide table — scroll it sideways for the ages, the registers and the trades, with the names held at the left. Press any column head to sort by it, and again to turn it over; Korea stays at the top, being the total rather than one of the thirteen. Manchukuo has one map, its population in 1943, and it is the one place where the shading and the shapes are of different years: the provinces here are traced from a 1935 sheet and there were fourteen of them then, where the 1943 report has nineteen. Five had been made out of those fourteen in between and none of them out of a single parent, so they cannot be put back — they are in the table with no shape of their own, each card says which of the two years its outline and its figure belong to, and every density is the report's own rather than one worked out over the polygon. The classes are the report's too: it drew this map itself and the key uses its five steps. At the foot of each table are the other tables — the other date, and the cities — and under them the two dates set against each other on the figures they share, with the change and the percentage worked out. The columns are named for the year the figures are of rather than the map they are drawn on, so Taiwan's reads 1930 and 1941: its later return is the register at the end of 1941. Where the two dates do not count the same thing the same way the table says so, and a row whose two figures are over different ground carries a dagger and an explanation under it — 臺東廳 and 花蓮港廳, which the 1930 return gives as the coastal strip the map draws and the 1941 one as the whole prefecture.
* **Railways beside the map** - Zoom in to ground a railway crossed and a track button appears under the reset, with the station button beside it: Korea's railways over Korea, Taiwan's over Taiwan. It is the same switch as the one in **Layers**, and it is filled when the line is drawn.
* **Sugar railways** - With Taiwan's railways on and the train tools away, zoom in to the island and a cane-stalk button appears beside the zoom controls. It draws the sugar company lines of 1929: 762 mm plantation track, thousands of kilometres of it across the western plain, under the government railway and much quieter than it.
* **Annotations** - Draw your own marks over the map, name them, describe them and share them. See **Drawing your own annotations** below.
* **Links** - If you pan or zoom to a location, and copy the URL, it will keep the bounding box information in the link allowing the person you are sharing the link with to view roughly the same location you are looking at. Useful if you want to share a specific view. Click on the page title in the top left to load the default view.


### Drawing your own annotations

Press **Create** at the top of the screen, or open **Layers** and press **Create annotations**. Nothing is sent anywhere: the file is written by your own browser and read back by it, and a map you load stays on your machine. It works on a phone, but a desktop or a tablet is easier.

**Locked and unlocked.** Marks that arrive with a shared link open **locked**: they can be read by clicking but a stray press cannot move them. A pencil button at the right of the map unlocks the tools. Your own marks open unlocked, and the small padlock at the top of the panel locks them — worth doing before showing a set to a class, so that a misplaced click does not redraw the lesson.

* **The five tools** - **Point** places a single mark, **Arrow** takes a start and an end, **Line** takes as many corners as you like, **Area** closes into a shape, and **Text** drags out a box of writing. Press a tool once and it draws one thing and then steps back; **press it a second time and it stays out** until you put it away — a tool left out is marked with a thick line under it.
* **Finishing and undoing** - For a line or an area, **Enter** finishes the shape and so does the **Finish** button; **Escape** abandons it. **Backspace** takes back the last corner while you are drawing, and removes the selected mark when you are not. **Undo** takes back one step of anything — a shape, a move, a deletion, or a burst of typing — and there is no redo, so it is worth saving before an experiment.
* **Naming and describing** - A **Name** is written on the map beside the mark, and can be kept off it for one mark without hiding the rest. A **Short note** is what the pointer shows. A **Description** is the longer account, and it opens in the panel on the right when the mark is clicked.
* **Dates** - Optional **Start** and **End**, read leniently: `1937`, `Sept 1931` and `1941-12-08` all work. Once two dates are in play, a small clock appears on the map beside the zoom buttons: **‹** and **›** step through the dates at which something changes, showing only the marks that are in scope at that moment — together with every mark that carries no date at all, so the background of the argument stays put. **▶** runs it, two seconds a stage, and **×** puts everything back. The map does not move while it runs: you set the view, and the shapes appear and disappear over it.
* **Style** - Colour, weight, opacity, and for an area a separate fill. A **Point** can be one of twenty-five shapes, including military unit symbols and formation sizes. A **Line** can be dashed six ways and can label its own length, leg by leg or as a total. An **Arrow** can be curved, and can end in a solid head, a barbed one, an open one, a dot, or a bar meaning an advance that was stopped.
* **Text** - Drag out a box for a note. The **Description** fills it and the **Name** becomes a bold heading at the top; with no name there is no heading and the description starts at the top instead. Its three colours — text, box and border — can all be changed, and the size a little. Drag the corner to resize it, or anywhere in the middle to move it.
* **Whether a text box belongs to the ground** - **Scales** decides what happens when you zoom, and it is on to begin with. **On**, the box belongs to the ground it covers: pull back and it shrinks, its words go when they get too small to read, and eventually it becomes a small square that behaves like a city — still there, still clickable, and it opens back out when you zoom in. **Off**, it keeps its size on screen however far you zoom, like a caption pinned to the map. Switching between the two never changes how the box looks at that moment; it only changes what happens next.
* **Smooth** - Lines and areas can have their corners rounded off, by eight degrees. The points you placed stay on the line; only the pieces between them bend, so smoothing never moves a corner you put somewhere deliberately.
* **Approximate areas** - An area can have a **Blurred** edge, which says "about here" rather than drawing a frontier the source never had.
* **Editing** - Click a mark to select it. **Drag a corner** to move that corner; **drag the middle of an area, a text box or a line** to move the whole thing; **right-click a corner** to remove it, and press **Duplicate** for another one like it. Shift-drag a box over several marks to select them together.
* **Where a set should be looked at from** - **Set default view** at the top of the panel remembers the frame the map is showing now, and saves it with the annotations. Anyone who opens the file or the link starts there, and once they wander off, a button appears under the map's own reset control to bring them back to it.
* **Keeping it** - **Save file** writes a GeoJSON file. **Copy link** puts the whole set into a web address if it is small enough, and a counter above the buttons says how close you are. **Add file…** merges another set into this one rather than replacing it.
* **Sharing** - Someone who opens your link sees the marks locked, so a stray press cannot move them, and can read any of them by clicking. A pencil button unlocks the tools. Their own annotations are set aside, not overwritten, and one press brings them back.
* **A warning about large files** - This is a drawing tool, not a GIS. A GeoJSON exported from QGIS can carry tens of thousands of points in a single shape, and loading one will make the whole map slow to draw, slow to pan and slow to answer — on a school laptop or a phone, badly so. Simplify a layer before bringing it here, or cut it down to the part you actually want to talk about. Anything up to a few hundred marks is comfortable.
