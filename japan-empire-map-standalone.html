About this map

This interactive map is for practising the geography of the Japanese Empire: the home islands, the formal colonies, the occupied territories and puppet states, the neighbouring countries, and the cities, ports and battlefields that recur in the history of modern Japan from Perry's arrival in 1853 to the surrender in 1945.

How to use it

  • 1930 / Dec 1942 — which map you are looking at. In 1930 the colours show whose empire each place belonged to, on the eve of the Manchurian Incident. In December 1942 they show how Japan held what it had taken, at the greatest extent of the empire.
  • Explore — hover over, or tap, any territory or marker to see what it is, what it was called, when it changed hands, and why it matters.
  • Quiz — you are asked to find a place and you click it on the map. Your score and the places you missed are reported at the end.
  • Basic / Intermediate / Advanced — how many places the quiz asks about, and how many names appear on the map. Every territory stays clickable at every level.
  • English / 日本語 / 中文 / 한국어 — which name is shown. English gives the name as it was used at the time, with the present-day name after it where they differ: Mukden (Shenyang), Taihoku (Taipei), Peking (Beijing), Batavia (Jakarta). Japanese gives the imperial-period form — Keijō for Seoul, Shinkyō for Changchun, Shōnantō for Singapore. Chinese is in traditional characters, as the period used them; Korean is in hangul with McCune-Reischauer romanisation. Explanatory notes stay in English throughout, as do the names of the provinces of Siam and the Philippines, which is how the period's own maps of them are lettered.

A caution about borders

Inside China the boundaries are the real provinces of 1928–45, so Manchukuo is Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang and Jehol as they actually stood, and Mengchiang is Chahar and Suiyuan. Karafuto is Sakhalin south of the 50th parallel, which is where the border ran, and the Kwantung Leased Territory is cut across the Liaodong isthmus on the line of the 1898 lease. Elsewhere, modern coastlines and outlines stand in for historical ones — fine for coasts, rough for land borders.

The one deliberately coarse thing is the area of occupied China, which is drawn as whole provinces and labelled as approximate. Japanese control there ran along the railways and around the cities, and much of the countryside inside the line was held by Communist and Nationalist guerrillas. The coastal enclaves outside it — Canton, Amoy, Hainan — are marked as cities rather than territory.

Hovering names the sub-unit under the pointer as well as the country: the province, prefecture, residency, state, division or island. These are the units of the period and they carry their period names — Kankyōhoku-dō rather than North Hamgyŏng, Tayabas rather than Quezon, Chiengmai rather than Chiang Mai, Celebes rather than Sulawesi. Their outlines, though, are assembled from modern ones, so where a boundary has moved since, the shape is an approximation and the name is the thing to trust. Four places to keep in mind. Manchukuo redrew the three north-eastern provinces into nineteen of its own between 1934 and 1941; they are drawn here as Fengtien, Kirin and Heilungkiang, the provinces Manchukuo was made out of. British India's provinces were interleaved with several hundred princely states, and only the largest of those — Hyderabad, Kashmir, Mysore, Travancore and Cochin, and the Rajputana agency — are separated out, so the rest are drawn inside whichever province surrounded them. Korea's thirteen provinces are recovered by putting the post-war units back where they came from, which cannot restore Kaesong to Keiki-dō. And Siam's changwat sat inside the monthon, the "circles" abolished in 1933, which are not drawn.

Where several modern states made up one historical territory they are drawn as a single shape with no boundary between them, because there was none: British India has no line at the Punjab, French Indochina none at the Mekong, Korea none at the 38th parallel. Present-day borders that had not been drawn yet do not appear.

The front line moved constantly between 1931 and 1945, and any single date is a snapshot. For close work, use a proper historical atlas.
