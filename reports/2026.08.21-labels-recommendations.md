# Labels, names and the overview text

**Date:** 21 August 2026
**Scope:** the words only — `texts/` in full (names, date phrases, notes, legend, epoch blurbs, About and Sources), the reader-facing strings in `index.html` and `map.js`, and the naming policy as `texts/README.md` states it. No geometry, no code, no performance.
**Method:** Part 1 lifts every text-related item out of `reports/2026.08.17-fact-check-recommendations.md` and `reports/2026.08.17-general-recommendations.md` and re-checks each one against the file it now lives in — `texts/` did not exist when those reports were written, and their line references into `data.js` no longer resolve. Part 2 is a fresh reading of the corpus. Nothing in the repository was modified except this file.

Three general remarks.

First, the move to `texts/` has already done what it was built to do. The Sources page and `SOURCES.md` are one file now, the naming policy is written down in a place an editor will find it, and the generator refuses rather than guesses. Several of the August fact-check's items have been fixed in passing.

Second, the failure mode has changed shape. It used to be *drift between three hand-kept copies*. It is now **drift between the label and the note that sits under it**, and **drift between the 1930 record and the 1942 record of the same place**. Nine of the fixes below are half-landed: the prose was corrected and the `when` phrase beside it was not, or the 1942 note was corrected and the 1930 note was not. Those are worth doing first, because a student who notices one has been handed two answers on one screen.

Third, the About dialog is now the least reliable text on the map. It describes a Manchukuo the map does not draw, a Mengchiang the map stopped drawing, and a naming rule that is the reverse of the rule the data follows and the reverse of the rule the Sources page states three clicks away. It is also the first prose most readers will meet.

## Part 2 — New findings

### 2.1 The About panel describes a Manchukuo and a Mengchiang the map does not draw

**Where:** `texts/pages/about.md:19` and `about.md:23`, against `sub-units/manchukuo.csv` and `sub-units/mengjiang.csv`.

About says, of Manchukuo: *"they are drawn here as Fengtien, Kirin and Heilungkiang, the provinces Manchukuo was made out of."*
The map draws **fourteen** provinces, from the South Manchuria Railway's own 1935 sheet — the four Hsingan provinces, Heiho, Lungkiang, Sankiang, Pinkiang, Chientao, Fengtien, Antung, Kirin, Chinchow and Jehol. There is no Heilungkiang among them. `manchukuo.md:3` says so plainly. The same About sentence also says the redrawing produced "nineteen" provinces "between 1934 and 1941", while the sheet the map actually uses is a fourteen-province sheet of 1935.

About says, of Mengchiang: *"Mengchiang is Chahar and Suiyuan."*
The map draws it from its own traced boundary and names three federated governments — the Mongol leagues, the Chanan administration and the Jinbei administration (`mengjiang.csv`, `1942.md:83`). `1942.md:77` states explicitly that it is "Drawn from its own traced boundary now, not from two provinces standing in for it."

**Proposed:** rewrite both sentences from what the data now holds. Manchukuo: fourteen provinces off the 1935 SMR sheet, with Jehol added in 1933, and the Republic's Liaoning/Kirin/Heilungkiang left to serve 1930. Mengchiang: a traced boundary, and the three governments the federation of September 1939 joined.

**Confidence:** high. Verified against the CSVs.

### 2.2 About and Sources state opposite naming rules, and About states the wrong one

**Where:** `texts/pages/about.md:15` against `texts/pages/sources.md:121`.

About: *"giving the name as it was used at the time with the present-day one after it where they differ: **Mukden (Shenyang)**, Taihoku (Taipei), **Peking (Beijing)**, Batavia (Jakarta)."*

Sources: *"Everywhere in China it is the other way round, and in Pinyin with its tone marks — **Shěnyáng (Mukden)**, Chóngqìng (Chungking), **Běijīng (Peking / Peiping)** …"*

The data does what Sources says: `sites.csv:24` is `Shěnyáng (Mukden)`, `sites.csv:32` is `Běijīng (Peking / Peiping)`. About's two Chinese examples are the only two examples it gives that the map contradicts, and they are the two a student is most likely to check.

**Proposed:** replace About's bullet with a condensed version of the Sources paragraph, keeping Taihoku and Batavia and swapping Mukden and Peking round. While there: About says "McCune-Reischauer" with a hyphen where Sources says "McCune–Reischauer" with an en dash.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.3 Korean names: two romanisation systems in one directory

**Where:** `sub-units/korea-islands.csv` against `sub-units/korea.csv`.

`korea.csv` is faultless McCune–Reischauer throughout: `Keiki-dō (Kyŏnggi-do)`, `Chūseihoku-dō (Ch'ungch'ŏngbuk-to)`, `Heianhoku-dō (P'yŏnganbuk-to)`.

`korea-islands.csv`, the file next to it, is Revised Romanisation throughout — **Ulleungdo, Jukdo, Gwaneumdo, Seodo, Dongdo** — and the `ko` column repeats the Revised form inside the bracket where the policy asks for MR: `울릉도 (Ulleungdo)` rather than `울릉도 (Ullŭngdo)`.

This is A24 living in the map's own labels rather than in its documentation. It is also the file whose commentary says "Korean first here, because these are Korean-administered today and the Korean names are what a reader will meet" — which is the argument *for* getting the romanisation right, not a licence to change system.

**Proposed:** Ullŭngdo, Chukto, Kwanŭmdo, Sŏdo, Tongdo, with the Revised forms in brackets if the modern-lookup argument is worth keeping; and fix the `ko` brackets to match. Or state a Revised-Romanisation exception in About and Sources and apply it consistently, including to `sources.md:24`'s Jagang and Jeju.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.4 One place, two names, on one map

Four collisions a student can walk into. None is a factual error; each is a label that will match the wrong thing.

- **Canton.** `1942.md:526` — "refugees from Hong Kong and Canton" — is Guangzhou. `1942.md:280` — "Canton, already an airline base under the joint Anglo-American administration agreed in 1939" — is Canton Island in the Phoenix group, 8,000 km away. The same file, 250 lines apart, unqualified in both places. `1930.md:270` has the island again.
- **Christmas Island.** The record (`malaya-borneo.csv:15`, `overrides-1942.csv:15`) is the Indian Ocean island. `1942.md:278` — "American troops landed on Christmas Island and Fanning in February 1942" — is Kiritimati in the Line Islands. A reader who follows that sentence to the map lands on the wrong ocean.
- **Shinkyō.** `sites.csv:25` gives Changchun as `新京 (Shinkyō)`, and `1930.csv:15` / `1942.csv:59` give Sinkiang as `新疆 (Shinkyō)`. Both readings are correct — 新京 and 新疆 are homophones — but Sources at `:121` singles out "新京 Shinkyō for Changchun" as an example of the naming rule, which makes the collision look like an error even though it is not.
- **Weihai.** `sites.csv:38` heads the marker `Wēihǎi (Weihaiwei)` while its own `zh` cell reads `威海衛 (Wēihǎiwèi)`, and the territory underneath it, `1930.csv:57`, heads `Wēihǎiwèi (Weihaiwei)`. The marker and the territory it sits on give the pinyin two different ways.

**Proposed:** "Canton Island" and "Christmas Island (Kiritimati)" in the two Pacific notes; a clause on the Sinkiang record or in Sources noting the homophone; and one pinyin form for Weihaiwei.

**Confidence:** high on all four.

### 2.6 Eight notes serve both epochs verbatim, and three of them are anachronistic on 1930

`goa`, `pondicherry`, `princelystates`, `tuva`, `nepal`, `sikkim`, `bhutan` and `other` are byte-identical in `1930.md` and `1942.md`.

Five are timeless and fine. Three are not:

- **`goa`** — "Portugal was neutral, and it stayed Portuguese until India took it by force in 1961." On the 1930 sheet there is no war to be neutral in, and the sentence reaches thirty-one years past the map's date.
- **`pondicherry`** — "They declared for the Free French in 1940 and were transferred to India in the 1950s." Same problem, ten and twenty years out.
- **`princelystates`** — "eleven provinces ruled directly" is the post-1937 figure (A13). In 1930 it is nine governors' provinces and a handful of chief commissioners' ones.

The map is elsewhere careful about this: `overrides-1930.md` exists precisely so that "a place on the 1930 map should not be described by something that had not happened yet". These three slipped through because they are territory notes and the override machinery for territories is per-epoch files rather than an override sheet.

**Proposed:** split `goa`, `pondicherry` and `princelystates` into two notes each. The forward look is a device the map uses deliberately elsewhere ("Guadalcanal would become the turning point", `1930.md:193`), so the 1930 versions can keep a closing clause — but in the future conditional the rest of the 1930 file uses, not the past tense.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.8 The `orig` column is documented as hidden and is not

**Where:** `texts/pages/sources.md:123`, `texts/README.md`, against `map.js:179`.

Sources tells the reader: *"A fifth field, `orig`, holds the local endonym where it is neither Chinese nor Korean … It is kept for reference and **is not shown in the interface**."* `texts/README.md` says the same.

`map.js:179` builds the alternate-names line from `['orig', 'ja', 'zh', 'ko', 'en']`, with `orig` **first**. Thai, Vietnamese, Malay, Burmese, Uyghur, Mongolian, Russian, Hawaiian, Aleut, Tagalog, Okinawan and Taiwanese all reach the tooltip. That is a good feature; it is simply the opposite of what the page says, and it means About's "Names in four scripts" undersells the map by a whole column.

Two consequences worth fixing at the same time:

- **The `ko` column is almost empty because Korean names were filed in `orig`.** `sites.csv` has a `ko` cell in **1 row of 78** — the Coral Sea, of all things — while Seoul, Pusan, Inchon, Pyongyang and Kanghwa carry their hangul in `orig`. `1930.csv` and `1942.csv` have one `ko` each (`chosen`). It reads correctly today because `orig` is shown; it will break the moment anything keys on `ko` — a language filter, an export, or the `lang` attributes the August review asked for.

**Proposed:** correct the two "not shown" sentences; add the fifth field to About's names bullet; move the Korean cells from `orig` to `ko`; and either add the Sino-Korean readings Sources describes or trim the claim to what the data holds.

**Confidence:** high on the "not shown" error and the column counts.

### 2.11 Names never carry a `lang` attribute

`index.html` has four `lang="ja"` spans, all on the three Japanese security-classification terms in the North China Area Army panel — which is exactly right, and shows the author knows the mechanism. `map.js` has none. Every Japanese, Chinese and Korean name in every tooltip and detail card — several thousand strings — is unmarked, so a screen reader reads them in the page language and the browser picks the fallback CJK font by locale rather than by content. On a map whose selling point is four scripts at once, that is the one place the markup should be exact.

This is the text half of the August review's B8 and can be done in `map.js` where the name spans are built.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.12 The two Sinkiang notes are written in a different voice from every other note

**Where:** `1930.md:94–99` and `1942.md:510–515`.

> "Xinjiang was formally a province of the Republic of China and Jin Shuren was recognised by Nanking as its provincial chairman. **In practice, however**, Jin's government controlled its own administration, armed forces, finances and external trade, while the central government exercised little day-to-day authority. **Xinjiang was not an independent state, but it lay largely outside effective Nationalist control.**"

Four things mark these two notes off from the other 130:

1. They call the province **Xinjiang** in bare pinyin, where the record's own label is `Xīnjiāng (Sinkiang)` and the note two records away (`1930.md:59`) says "Sinkiang".
2. They give the two governors as **Jin Shuren** and **Sheng Shicai**, in pinyin, in a corpus that otherwise says P'u-i, Wang Ching-wei, Fu Tso-yi and Chang Hsüeh-liang.
3. The register is hedged and clausal — "In practice, however", "By December, … was beginning to grow" — where every other note is declarative and short.
4. The 1930 one restates its own conclusion in a closing sentence, which nothing else in the corpus does.

**Proposed:** rewrite both to the house voice and to Sinkiang / Chin Shu-jen / Sheng Shih-ts'ai. `1942.md:608`'s "Wu Yuexing" is the third pinyin personal name and should go the same way, or the whole convention should be flipped to pinyin deliberately — but not left as it is.

**Confidence:** high on the inconsistency; the direction of the fix is the author's call.

### 2.14 Japanese words in the prose lose their macrons; ships lose their italics

**Macrons.** The prose is scrupulous — Tōgō, Saigō Takamori, Chōshū, Kantō, Hokkaidō, Tōjō, Ryūkyū, Keijō, Ōmiyajima, Ōtorishima — with two exceptions, both at `sites.md:373`: **Shoho** for Shōhō and **Shokaku** for Shōkaku. They are the only unmacronned Japanese words in the corpus.

**Italics.** `<em>` reaches the page verbatim and is used once, at `1930.md:225` (`<em>except Guam</em>`). No ship name is italicised: the *Lexington*, *Shōhō*, *Shōkaku*, *Zuikaku* (`sites.md:373`), the *Prince of Wales* and *Repulse* (`sites.md:315`), the *Emden* (`1930.md:355`). Nor is any romanised foreign term: *gyokusai* (`sites.md:447`), *monthon* and *changwat* (`about.md:23`). About's own book list *is* italicised, because that page is rendered from Markdown.

**Proposed:** the two macrons are unarguable. Italics are a house-style decision, but ships are the standard case and there are only six of them.

**Confidence:** high on the macrons.

### 2.15 Straight and curly quotation marks are mixed

47 curly apostrophes against 7 straight ones in shown prose. The seven:

- `1930.md:454` — "Sikkim's"
- `1942.md:352` — "map's", "Tulagi's"
- `1942.md:561` — "Sikkim's"
- `1942.md:598` — "sheet's"
- `1942.md:606` — "army's"
- `1942.md:608` — "Wu Yuexing's"

Double quotes run the other way: straight everywhere (18 instances) except `1942.md:529`, "advisers", which is curly.

`sources.md` and `about.md` are wholly straight, which is correct — they are rendered from Markdown and can be typographically converted at build time. The other files ship verbatim, so what is typed is what appears.

**Proposed:** curly throughout the verbatim files, straight throughout the two rendered pages, and a check in `tools/build_texts.py` that refuses a straight apostrophe in a verbatim field. Every one of these was a real slip and each hid in a long file.

**Confidence:** high. Counted mechanically.

### 2.16 Units and figures are inconsistent

**Distance and area.** Metric with numerals in Asia — 1,000 km (`1930.md:15`), 340 km (`1930.md:377`), 500 km (`1942.md:364`), 87 km (`korea-islands.md:18`), 110 km (`ryukyus.csv:24`). Imperial spelled out in the Pacific — ninety miles (`1930.md:228`), six hundred miles (`1930.md:255`, `1942.md:282`), eighteen-thousand-mile (`sites.md:80`). Areas are worse: `1930.md:210` puts "about 2,100 km² of land scattered across three million square miles of ocean" in a single sentence, and Tarawa is two square kilometres in one file and three square miles in another (see Part 1, B8).

**Numerals against words.** Large figures are numerals throughout — 80,000 troops, 2,400 killed, 140,000, 100,000, 55,000, 70,000 — except "Ninety-eight" (`sites.md:408`, and lower-case at `1942.md:230`), "three hundred and fifty" (`1942.md:347`), and "Seventy-six hours" at `sites.md:417` against "76 hours" at `1942.md:241`, which is the same fact in the same two units of style.

**Proposed:** one distance system, stated once; numerals for anything above a hundred; and pick one figure for Tarawa.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.17 Notes duplicated where a reader will see them twice

- **Funafuti.** `overrides-1942.md` and `1942.md:264` say the same two facts in near-identical words, and the sub-unit's note shows underneath its parent's.
- **The Liancourt Rocks.** `korea-islands.md` gives `## Seodo` and `## Dongdo` **byte-identical** four-sentence notes. The two islets are 150 m apart and will often both be under the pointer at the zoom that reveals them.
- **The Senkakus.** `senkaku.md` gives the same single sentence — "Claimed today by Japan, by the People's Republic of China and by Taiwan; uninhabited, and administered by Japan" — to all four records, none of which says anything about the island it names. And `senkaku.csv:4` and `:5` are the same island twice: keys `Kuba-shima` and `Kuba Island`, with identical `en`, `ja` and `zh`.
- **Sukarno.** `sites.md:353` and `1942.md:159` both say he "chose to work with the occupation".

**Proposed:** `snippets.md` exists for exactly this. `{{liancourt}}` and `{{senkaku}}` would say each caution once; the Senkaku records can then each carry a line about themselves, and the duplicate Kuba row can be resolved or explained.

**Confidence:** high.

### 2.18 Dead override rows

Nine cells in `sites/overrides-1930.csv` restate the base value exactly and change nothing: `tokyo.date`, `kyoto.date`, `seoul.date`, `harbin.date`, `changchun.orig`, `singapore.orig`, `rangoon.ja`, `rangoon.orig`, `rangoon.zh`. `overrides-1930.md`'s `## seoul` note is likewise byte-identical to the base note in `sites.md`.

Harmless in output, but they make the override files look as though the 1930 map differs in ten places where it differs in one, and they are the kind of thing a later editor changes in one copy only.

**Proposed:** delete them, and have `build_texts.py` warn on an override that equals its base — the same class of refusal the generator already makes elsewhere.

**Confidence:** high.
