### Left for a decision

- **Tianjin's "eight foreign concessions."** Nine were granted; eight is right only for 1902–17, after the American merged into the British. Stated consistently in two files, so it looks like a decision rather than a slip — but a reader meeting "nine" elsewhere will query it.
- **Manchukuo's fourteen provinces on a December 1942 map.** Fourteen is the 1935 sheet; the state had sixteen by 1937 and nineteen by 1941. The provenance is disclosed honestly and About now explains it, but no note says the scheme later grew.
- **Tsushima, "the first modern defeat of a European power by an Asian one."** A textbook line, contestable in kind and in fact. A wording question, not a date.
- **The Ellice: "nine atolls" in the prose, eight rows in the data.** Nine is right — Niulakita is the ninth, uninhabited then. The prose is sound and the data is short an island.
- **1930 notes narrating post-1930 events in the past tense** — the mandate's 1941 landing and 1944 recapture, the Line Islands settlement scheme, the US guano colonists. All dated, so nobody is misled about when, but they read as accomplished fact where the map elsewhere uses "would become".
- **Thailand "rewarded" with Cambodian and Lao territory.** Those came from the Franco-Thai settlement of May 1941, months before the December alliance, as the neighbouring note correctly says. Only Malaya and Burma were rewards.
- Four smaller points: the Spratly label saying "annexed by France, April 1930" where the note correctly says occupied; the Paracels folded into the 30 March 1939 annexation; Mengjiang "built out of Chahar and Suiyuan" two sentences before Jinbei comes out of Shanxi; and the Philippines' "commonwealth with promised independence from 1935" reading, on a 1930 map, as though it already existed.

### Verified sound

The reviewer checked and confirmed every date in `sites.csv` and the whole China browse layer; the 1930 and 1942 territory notes across the Pacific, Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean; the 47 prefectures, 13 Korean provinces, 49 Philippine provinces; the Kuriles including the post-surrender Soviet landings; and the internal consistency of the Okinawa, Nauru, Tarawa and North China Area Army figures against `SOURCES.md`.

## Part 2 — Locations a student would expect and cannot find

### The shape of the problem

Four structural gaps, in order of size. All four are confirmed against the built `data.js`.

**1. The 1930 map has four events on it.** `map.js:28` sets `EPOCH_1930_CUTOFF = 1931`, and `siteInEpoch` hides any site with a later `year`. Of 32 event markers, exactly four survive on the 1930 sheet — Uraga 1853, Kanghwa 1876, Tsushima 1905 and Nikolaevsk 1920 — against 32 on the 1942 sheet. Everything a course turns on for the 1920s and 30s — Huanggutun, the Salt March, Yen Bai, Musha, the Jiangxi Soviet, the rice riots — has no marker on the date it belongs to. The 1930 map is a map of territory with almost no history happening on it.

**2. The colonies carry no violence.** Taiwan has nine places across the two layers and **zero** events, ever. Korea has seven sites, and its only two dated events are 1876 and 1905 — nothing from the colonial period at all. Japan proper has thirty places on the map and forty-six more already proposed. This is the sharpest asymmetry on the map.

**3. British India has twenty-two dots and not one event.** Confirmed: the site layer contains **no Indian record at all**. Every Indian place is a context city. The frame includes 1930 — the most eventful year in the Indian nationalist calendar — and the Bengal famine.

**4. Whole subjects have no location anywhere.** Chemical warfare, civilian internment, wartime famine, penal colonies, forced labour inside Japan. The map's own prose says these matter; nothing marks them.

### One correction to the reviewer's framing

The gazetteer is better stocked than the gap list implies: of 446 rows, **226 are already flagged `proposed`** — Pingfang, Hŭngnam, Yenangyaung, Miri, Kanchanaburi, Thanbyuzayat, Sandakan, Hollandia, Garapan, Cam Ranh and Changde among them. Most obvious dots are known and merely unbuilt. What follows is restricted to places in **neither** the map **nor** the proposed list, plus events that would land on a marker that already exists.

One thing the reviewer raised is **not** a gap, and I repeated it before checking: Hong Kong is drawn as a **territory with its own polygon** on both sheets. The `proposed` row in the gazetteer is a redundant city dot, not a missing place.

## Part 3 — What was implemented on this branch

### The event rule

Events used to answer to a single cutoff: anything after 1931 was hidden on the 1930 sheet and everything showed on the 1942 one. That left the 1930 map with **four events** and the 1942 map carrying incidents from twenty years before its date.

`siteInEpoch` now works in two windows, with a spine across both:

- **Cities are unchanged** — a place that existed by 1930 is on the 1930 map, everything is on the 1942 one.
- **Events belong to their decade** — 1910–1930 on the 1930 sheet, 1931 onward on the 1942 sheet.
- **A spine of six shows on both**: Uraga 1853, Kanghwa 1876, Tsushima 1905, the Mukden Incident 1931, the Marco Polo Bridge 1937, Pearl Harbor 1941. These are the hinges the two dates are points on.

The spine is a new `both` column in `sites.csv`, so widening or narrowing it is a data edit, not a code change. **Six is a first cut and the obvious thing to argue with** — the case for adding Midway, Guadalcanal or Nanjing is as good as the case against.

### The result

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|

| events on the 1942 sheet | 32 | **39** |

Checked in a browser: Musha, Dandi, Huanggutun and Amritsar draw on 1930 and are hidden on 1942; Buna–Gona, Savo and Huayuankou the reverse; Uraga and Pearl Harbor on both.

### Added

**Interwar events, which the 1930 sheet had almost none of** — Musha (27 October 1930, the month of the map's own date), Huanggutun (1928), Dandi and the Salt March (April 1930), the Chittagong armoury raid (April 1930), Yen Bai (February 1930), Amritsar (1919), Cheam-ri (1919), the Kwangju student movement (1929), Tapani (1915), the Uozu rice riots (1918).

**War events** — Huayuankou (the dikes cut, June 1938), Changkufeng/Lake Khasan (1938), Pingdingshan (1932), Poch'ŏnbo (1937), Buna–Gona and Milne Bay and Savo (1942), Midnapore and the Bengal famine.

**Six places that carry a subject the map was silent on** — Ōkunoshima (the army's gas plant), the Sup'ung dam, Ruijin (the Jiangxi Soviet), Côn Sơn and Boven Digoel (the two colonial penal systems), Ashio (industrial pollution and the first environmental protest in Japan).

That closes the three sharpest asymmetries the coverage review found: Taiwan and Korea now carry colonial-period violence, and British India has events for the first time.
