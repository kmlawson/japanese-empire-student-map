# Pan and zoom: where the time goes, and what could be done about it

**19 August 2026.** Three independent reviews, run in parallel and without sight of each
other: one reading the renderer (`map.js`, `styles.css`, `index.html`), one reading the
build output (`tools/build_map.py` and the four SVGs), one profiling the running page on
desktop and on an emulated phone. Nothing here has been implemented. Nothing in the repo
was modified by any of them.

Where the three agree, the finding is stated once. Where they contradict each other —
and on two substantial points they do — the contradiction is set out rather than
smoothed over, with a note on which evidence should be believed.

---

## The short version

Pan and zoom are **not** slow because of the JavaScript, the label engine, the
clip-paths, the hatch patterns, the hit-test overlays, the gazetteer's 3,149 nodes, or
the 2.9 MB of SVG sitting in the DOM. All of those measured at or inside the noise floor.

They are slow for two reasons, and the second is multiplied by the first:

1. **Every pan frame rewrites the `viewBox`, which invalidates every tile in the layer.**
   Measured: 21.3 of 21.3 tiles re-rastered per frame, at every frame, forever. A
   promoted CSS transform re-rasters 2.1. Desktop: 198 ms/frame → 1.5. Phone: 94 → 1.6,
   or by the other agent's tile-level measurement 268 → 28.
2. **85–88% of what is rastered is the 1.3 px round-jointed stroke drawn round every
   polygon.** Removing every stroke takes a frame to 15% of baseline on desktop and 13%
   on mobile — and the sea-and-UI floor is itself 13%. The land's *fills* are worth about
   two percentage points. 765 fill-only paths carrying 36 k commands are free; 144
   stroked paths carrying 90 k commands are the entire bill.

Everything else in this document is small by comparison, and several things that looked
large turned out not to be.

## What was measured

| | renderer lens | build lens | profiling lens |
|---|---|---|---|
| method | read `map.js` etc., then A/B with injected CSS, uncapped rAF | scripts over the committed SVGs; no build run | CDP tracing + `RasterTask` totals + sampling profiler, desktop and 4×-throttled mobile |
| headline instrument | ms/frame, interleaved A/B/A/B | vertices, bytes, coverage rasters | raster-ms per frame, **tiles rastered per frame** |

**Both measuring agents warn that their absolute numbers are inflated** — headless-shell
with software rasterisation, on a machine that was running all three of these reviews at
once. One of them put a CPU canary in front of every row and watched it climb from 14 ms
to 42 ms over ten minutes of sustained benchmarking. **Ratios inside an interleaved or
sandwiched block are the trustworthy part; absolutes are not.** Two independent cold runs
agreed to within 2% on the two views they shared, which is the best evidence available
that the ratios are sound.

## The regression the map has been carrying

1930 → 1942 over identical ground, desktop, admin on: **70.0 → 99.4 raster-ms per frame,
60 fps → 38 fps, 23 → 30 ms per pan step.** The cause is not more geometry:

| in the viewport at `bbox=105,20,130,42` | 1930 + admin | 1942 + admin + extent |
|---|---:|---:|
| path commands | 117,413 | 126,713 (+8%) |
| **of which stroked** | 82,039 across 143 paths | 90,265 across 144 paths (+10%) |
| fill-only | 35,374 across 765 paths | 36,448 across 765 paths |
| pan raster/frame | 70.0 ms | 99.4 ms (**+42%**) |

+10% stroked geometry buys +42% raster, because the occupied zone lays a second stroked,
hatched shape over ground that is already painted. It is overdraw of *stroked* pixels.
This was attributed from the geometry census rather than by direct ablation — the
variant that would have isolated `#a-occupiedzone` alone came back inside the noise band.

## Findings, ranked

### Tier 1 — large, and visually identical

#### 1.1 Composited translate for pan instead of `viewBox` rewriting

`map.js:1257` writes `viewBox` on **every `pointermove`** (`map.js:1633-1638`), not once
per frame. Measured three ways:

| | viewBox | promoted CSS transform |
|---|---:|---:|
| desktop 1400×950 dpr2, fit view | 198.4 ms/f | **1.5** |
| desktop, deep zoom (Okinawa) | 190.1 | **1.2** |
| phone 390×844 dpr3 | 94.1 | **1.6** |
| desktop, tiles rastered per frame | 21.3 | **2.1** |
| phone, tiles per frame | 8.0 | **1.3** |
| phone, raster ms/f (adjacent repeats) | 272.1 / 264.5 | **28.0 / 28.8** |

Three things worth knowing before anyone tries it:

* **`will-change: transform` is load-bearing.** A CSS transform *without* it measured
  **151 ms** — *worse than the viewBox*, because the layer keeps repainting and the
  transform adds work on top.
* **An SVG `<g transform>` is not composited.** The wrapper-group variant measured 299.6
  against a viewBox pan of 300.3 taken seconds later. Chrome does not promote
  SVG-internal transforms. This is not a workaround.
* **The SVG box must be over-rendered** or a translate exposes unpainted ground. Today
  `#map-svg` is exactly viewport-sized (`styles.css:178`, `:183`) and `#map-container`
  has no `overflow:hidden` (`styles.css:165`). The change is: grow the box by a margin on
  each side, widen the viewBox to match so the picture does not move, add
  `overflow:hidden`, translate during the drag, commit to `viewBox` on `pointerup` or
  when the offset exceeds the margin.

**Quality:** a translate-only pan on a promoted layer is *pixel-identical* to what ships.
**A composited *zoom* is not** — a CSS scale resamples the previous raster, so the map is
soft while the finger or the wheel is moving, the way Google Maps behaves, sharpening
when the gesture settles. That is a change to how the map looks *during* a gesture, and
it is a cartographic judgement, not an engineering one. Pan and zoom can be decided
separately: pan on the composited path, zoom left on `viewBox`.

#### 1.2 `stroke-linejoin: round` → `miter` with `stroke-miterlimit: 2`

> **Implemented 19 August 2026** on `.atom`, `#backings path` and `#land path.coast`
> only. Re-measured on a quiet machine: **60–71% of baseline on desktop, 62–69% on
> mobile**, across four views and three sandwiched rounds each. Pixel-diffed over
> eight views including the spikiest coasts at the deepest zoom: 0.1–1.1% of pixels
> differ at all, 0.02–0.16% by more than 8 per channel, mean difference over the
> differing pixels about 3 per channel. The worst-differing crops are
> indistinguishable by eye. See `tasks.md`.

`styles.css:220` (`.atom`), `styles.css:264` (`#backings path`). A round join emits an arc
— four to eight segments — at every vertex, and there are **161,408 stroked vertices** in
the main file.

Three measurements, three methods, one direction:

| | round → miter |
|---|---|
| profiling lens, sandwiched, 3 rounds desktop | **62% of baseline** (0.67 / 0.62 / 0.60) |
| profiling lens, mobile | 67% |
| renderer lens, fit view | 167.2 → 124.0 ms (**74%**); bevel 129.6 |
| build lens (estimate only) | 5–9% of a frame |

The build lens's estimate was low; the two measured figures should be believed. Call it
**a quarter to a third of raster, for one line of CSS.** At a 1.3 px stroke the join
geometry is sub-pixel; `stroke-miterlimit: 2` makes a sharp spike fall back to bevel,
which is also sub-pixel. Verify with a screenshot diff on a spiky coast — the Ryukyus,
the Aleutians — at 1× and at max zoom.

#### 1.3 `applyView` recomputes the home view, with `getBBox()` on every atom, every frame

`map.js:1260`: `var home = defaultView();` — inside the single write path, on every
frame, to decide two booleans (the `zoomed-in` class and whether the reset button is
idle). `defaultView()` (`map.js:1188`) calls `containerSize()`, `activeBounds()`
(`map.js:987`, loops every atom calling `getBBox()`) and `clampView()`.

Instrumented, 40 wheel ticks, desktop:

```
applyView       4.46 ms each
 defaultView    4.26            <- almost all of applyView
  containerSize 0.57 × 7 per frame = 4.0 ms/frame
  activeBounds  0.23
rescale         3.14
applyGazetteer  0.41
gateLabels      0.17
placeLabels     0.00
```

**Seven `getBoundingClientRect()` calls per frame** (280 over 40 ticks). `containerSize`
costs 0.57 ms because `rescale()` has just written 1,641 transform attributes, so the
next rect read forces a synchronous layout of a 6,300-node document — read-after-write
thrash. Cache the rect (invalidate on the `resize` handlers already wired at
`map.js:446-447`) and cache `home` (invalidate on resize, epoch and layer change).

~4.5 ms/frame desktop, ~18 ms/frame on the throttled phone. Zero visual change.

#### 1.4 `#browse` is `display:none` for the entire life of the page

`buildBrowse()` (`map.js:566`) creates 170 groups × 2 circles — 340 nodes — and registers
170 entries in `scalables`. Both places that set its display (`map.js:1287`,
`map.js:2645`) test `(!JMAP.GAZ && browseVisible())`, and `JMAP.GAZ` is always defined,
`cities-gaz.js` being loaded unconditionally at `index.html:175`. So the dots never
appear, and `rescale()` writes a transform to all 170 of them on every zoom frame. (The
browse *labels* are still used over the gazetteer dots; only the dot groups are dead.)

More generally, `rescale()` (`map.js:1336`) writes to **1,641 elements, ~40% of which can
never be seen** at that moment: the dead browse layer, the gazetteer of the epoch not on
screen (both epochs are in `#gaz`; `applyGazetteer` hides one, `rescale` transforms
both), and the ~300 of 314 label texts that are `display:none`. A `scalablesLive` array
rebuilt on visibility change, or a single CSS custom property, removes it. Invisible.

#### 1.5 `pointer-events: none` on `#jmap` for the duration of a drag

Blink's own hit-test to find the event target costs **0.30 ms per `pointermove`**
(`EventHandler::handleMouseMoveEvent`, 36.5 ms over 123 moves), ~1–3 ms per pan step.
`map.js:2155` already short-circuits the app's `onHover` when `dragStart` is set — this
is the layer below that. Nothing is hoverable while you are dragging, so suppressing it
is invisible.

#### 1.6 `showTooltip` forces a full-document layout on every hover move over land

`map.js:2179` rebuilds the tooltip DOM and then reads
`tooltip.getBoundingClientRect()` at `map.js:2255`. Measured **4.55 ms/call** with admin
off, **6.23 ms** with admin on. A hover with no button down — `pick()` + `provinceAt()` +
`showTooltip()` — costs **3.4–3.9 ms per mousemove** of main-thread CPU, and four times
that on the throttled mobile profile (though a touch device never fires it). Position
from a cached height with a transform, or do the read inside a rAF.

Same class, smaller: `applyGazetteer()` (`map.js:1288`) runs on every rAF and loops all
890 entries writing `style.display`, though it depends only on three rarely-changing
values — 0.4 ms/frame, guard and return early.

#### 1.7 Relative path encoding — 36% of the bytes, bit-exact

`tools/build_map.py:3122` (`ring_to_path`), `:3146` (`line_to_path`), `:3089` (`fmt`).
Deltas between adjacent coastline vertices are 1–3 characters; absolute coordinates are
6–7. Rebuilding every `d` attribute as `M<abs> l<rel…>`:

| file | now | after | saved |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `japan-empire-map.svg` | 3,013,000 | 1,926,945 | **−36.0%** |
| `-admin.svg` | 1,090,106 | 683,350 | −37.3% |
| `-roc.svg` | 699,768 | 482,344 | −31.1% |
| `-fine.svg` | 587,447 | 439,653 | −25.2% |
| **all four** | **5,390,321** | **3,532,292** | **−1,814 KB** |
| gzipped, all four | 1,538,985 | 838,964 | −683 KB |

**Bit-exactness was verified, not assumed:** the encoder accumulates in fixed point, so
reconstructed absolutes are identical numbers. Round-tripped 165,897 points across 400
paths, **0 mismatches**. Not one drawn point moves.

Raster saving: **zero** — same vertices, same paint. The win is parse time (typically
150–400 ms cold for 2.9 MB of path text), memory, and network. Three JS sites parse `d`
as absolute and split on `M` — `map.js:2976` `boxesOf()`, the fine prune at
`map.js:3119-3126`, `admin.js:543-546` — so keep `M` absolute **per subpath**, which
preserves split-and-rejoin exactly; only `boxesOf()` needs to accumulate deltas. Do *not*
use a fully relative `m` per subpath: it saves a further 1.8 KB and breaks the fine
layer's subpath deletion, because with `m` every later subpath shifts.

#### 1.8 Two dead-weight items in the build output

* **The `ccp` backing is `display:none` in every state.** Emitted unconditionally at
  `build_map.py:5837`, hidden unconditionally at `styles.css:628`. 1,982 vertices, 28.4 KB
  raw, 10.9 KB gzipped, and its geometry is identical to the union of `#a-ccp`'s 19
  sub-units (rasterised at 0.25 units: 158,327 cells each way, 0 either-only). All four
  `backingEls['ccp']` uses in `map.js` are guarded.
* **The occupied zone is 752 `<path>` elements carrying 7,064 vertices** —
  `build_map.py:5942`, one per traced piece, median 5 vertices each, 700 of them ≤10, and
  only 5 distinct `data-prov` labels between them, 732 sharing one. All 752 rings have the
  same winding, so concatenating them is pixel-exact under nonzero fill (the repo already
  relies on this identity at `map.js:2405-2419`). 752 → 5 elements, −49 KB. The build lens
  estimated 5–12% of a frame from removing 747 clipped draw calls; **the profiling lens's
  clip measurement (below) makes that estimate look too high** — treat the payload saving
  as certain and the frame saving as unverified. One caveat: `.prov-hot`
  (`styles.css:319`) would then brighten the whole North China block instead of a single
  islet. Merging only the 732 same-label pieces keeps Canton, Swatow, Amoy and Hainan
  separate and still gets 752 → 5.

### Tier 2 — large, but the picture changes

#### 2.1 Stroke width and the hairline cliff — and a genuine disagreement

The renderer lens found a **cliff**, not a slope, at a *declared* width of 1.0:

| stroke-width | ms/frame, fit view |
|---:|---:|
| 1.30 (as built) | 152.3 |
| 1.05 | 147.3 |
| **1.00** | **36.2** |
| 0.90 | 36.2 |

and reported it as independent of `devicePixelRatio` (dpr 1: 189.3 → 41.4 at 1.0; dpr 2:
163.0 → 36.9; dpr 3: 163.5 → 44.8).

The profiling lens found a **superlinear curve** and read the threshold in *device*
pixels: 1.3 → 85 points of cost, 0.5 → 18, 0.1 → 13, with the note that 1.3 CSS px at
dsf 2 is 2.6 device px, 0.5 is 1.0 device px, "and at dsf 3 on a phone the threshold is
0.33 CSS px, so a phone always pays full tessellation".

**These cannot both be right**, and the difference matters: if the cliff is in declared
units it is one CSS edit; if it is in device pixels then a phone can never reach the fast
path and the whole lever is desktop-only. Neither agent tested the crossing point at
several dpr *and* several widths in one interleaved block, which is the experiment that
settles it. **Do that before anyone touches the width.**

Either way this is a **visible change**: `styles.css:217-219` says the 1.3 exists to
close the anti-aliasing hairlines between adjacent simplified polygons, and 1.0 is a 23%
thinner crack-filler. Neither agent tested whether cracks reopen. This is a cartographic
decision.

#### 2.2 `non-scaling-stroke` — expensive only at deep zoom, and one agent retracted

The renderer lens flagged this first, then **corrected itself**: once the replacement
width is computed properly (1.3 × k user units, where k is the ratio `rescale()` already
computes), `vector-effect: none` is *identical* at fit view — 198.4 vs 199.7, and 152 vs
170 in a second interleaved run. The profiling lens, measuring only at China fit view,
independently found `vector-effect: none` at **95% of baseline, inside the noise**.

Where it *does* cost is deep zoom, where Blink cannot cull or cache stroke geometry and
re-strokes the full path in device space every frame even though almost all of it is off
screen:

| deep zoom, Okinawa (viewBox width 28, k = 0.026) | ms/frame |
|---|---:|
| as built | 190.1 |
| `vector-effect: none`, `stroke-width: 1.3 × k` — *the same rendered width* | **14.7** |
| `stroke: none` | 4.5 |
| `#backings` hidden | 189.9 (no help at all) |

**13× at deep zoom, arithmetically the same line**, since `non-scaling-stroke` is
*defined* as "width in the host coordinate system". `rescale()` (`map.js:1336`) already
computes k and already runs only on zoom frames; it would set a `--k` custom property and
the CSS would say `stroke-width: calc(1.3 * var(--k))`. Dash arrays scale by the same
factor (`styles.css:334-335`), and the masks built in `outlineOf` hard-code the
vector-effect at `map.js:2430`.

Expected to be pixel-identical by construction, **but not demonstrated**: the pixel diff
that was run also stripped the vector-effect from markers, rings and label halos without
rescaling their widths, so its 0.13% differing pixels overstates the real change. Verify
across the zoom range before believing it.

#### 2.3 `#backings` is genuinely seen, so it cannot simply go

42 paths, **101,382 vertices, 1,365 KB — 44.4% of the main file and 45% of live path
commands.** Hiding it takes a fit-view pan from 53.2 → 22.3 ms desktop, 28.9 → 11.9 phone,
and measures 63% of baseline in the sandwiched run. But a pixel diff says it is doing
work:

```
#backings visibility:hidden   6.226% of pixels change (6.136% visibly)
#backings stroke:none         0.698% of pixels change (0.491% visibly)
```

So neither hiding it nor unstroking it is free. The route to deleting part of it is
1.9 below, not a display toggle.

### Tier 3 — structural, high effort, and the honest payoff

#### 3.1 Shared-arc topology, which would let ~290 KB of backings *and* the stroke go

This is the root cause of both the largest layer and the per-shape stroke, and the
generator says so itself (`build_map.py:5526-5537`, `:5849-5853`): adjacent sub-units
share an edge in the source and lose it to simplification, so a crack opens, so a filler
goes underneath and a stroke goes round everything.

For the atoms in `BACKING_FROM_SUBUNITS` (`build_map.py:720`) the backing is literally
`dissolve()` of the *same rings* the sub-units come from, re-simplified. Rasterised
against the sub-units sitting on them (0.25-unit grid):

| atom | backing verts | backing bytes | area not covered by its own sub-units | mean fringe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `philippines` | 14,787 | 205,769 | **1.2%** | 0.026 u = **1.0 px at 40×** |
| `newguinea_au` | 2,811 | 38,684 | 0.6% | 0.042 u |
| `northborneo` | 1,736 | 23,939 | 1.1% | 0.042 u |
| `malaya_thai` | 831 | 10,547 | 0.8% | 0.028 u |
| `chishima` | 592 | 7,551 | 1.6% | 0.021 u |
| `aleutians` | 1,670 | 19,779 | 10.4% | 0.114 u |
| `ryukyu` | 443 | 5,928 | 46.4% | 0.273 u |
| nine others | 2,082 | ~31,000 | **0.0% each** | — |

The Philippines backing is 205 KB and 14,787 vertices, painted and stroked every frame,
to supply a fringe averaging one device pixel at 40×. Four backings are byte-identical
`d` strings to their single sub-unit.

If shared boundary arcs were simplified **once** and the sub-units rebuilt from them
(TopoJSON-style quantise-and-share; `dissolve` at `build_map.py:1952` already finds
shared edges), the cracks close by construction — **and not one drawn point moves**. It
keeps exactly the vertices the simplifier already chose and only makes neighbours agree
on them. That is why it is the right answer where "simplify more" is not.

Honest limits: it helps ~290 KB of the 1,365 KB backing layer, not all of it — the
backings whose source is a genuinely different file from the sub-units (`china`, `dei`,
`princely`, `japan`, `korea`, `burma`, `siam`, `manchukuo`) still need a filler. And the
0.25-unit coverage raster cannot see a hairline; "0.0% uncovered" means no coarse-scale
difference, and the fringe-width column is the number that matters.

#### 3.2 `#seams` — 89% of it is painted and then covered up

`build_map.py:6029`, `push_seam` at `:2531`, `SEAM_MAX = 0.50` at `:2063`. Monte-Carlo,
800 interior points per seam, tested against everything drawn above it:

| seam | verts | bytes | **% actually exposed** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `indochina` | 7,391 | 89,865 | **4.1%** |
| `siamgain` | 4,779 | 60,201 | 10.2% |
| `siam` | 1,813 | 22,164 | 14.9% |
| `ussr` | 1,787 | 21,517 | 8.9% |
| `nepal` | 1,625 | 19,199 | 24.1% |
| `mongolia` | 1,173 | 13,810 | 24.6% |
| eleven others | 7,224 | 90,706 | 2.2–26.8% |
| **layer** | **25,792** | **318,462** | **11.0% area-weighted** |

The biggest seams are the most buried. The strips reach out to `SEAM_MAX = 0.50°` rather
than to the measured gap — on average about nine times wider than the crack they close.
Up to ~280 KB and 23,000 vertices. But the failure mode of getting it wrong is a visible
line of ocean between two countries somewhere nobody sampled, so this needs a
per-frontier measured maximum gap, not a smaller global constant. The seam cache
(`build_map.py:2320-2405`) at least makes iterating cheap. Worth noting the profiling
lens measured hiding `#seams` entirely at **98% of baseline** — this is a payload item,
not a frame-rate item.

#### 3.3 `clip-off-clients` is 100% duplicate geometry

`build_map.py:5758-5769`. 80,486 bytes, 5,516 vertices, three subpaths: a frame rectangle
plus **byte-identical** copies of the `manchukuo` and `mengjiang` backings, both already
in `#backings`. The comment at `:5747-5757` is right that a clip cannot subtract and
reordering cannot help — but a `<use href="#backing-manchukuo">` placed *above* the
occupied zone is a second instance rather than a reorder, at ~150 bytes instead of 80 KB.
It would need its own `--c` (a `use` inherits from its own position, and `map.js:838-847`
sets `--c` on `backingEls`), `pointer-events:none`, a `nostroke` class so the 1.3 px line
is not doubled, and a check on where the hatching then sits.

## Two things that were believed and are not true

**The big clip-paths are not a per-frame cost.** `clip-china` is 16,406 vertices and
233 KB; `clip-off-clients` 5,516 and 80 KB. The build lens estimated their removal at
2–15% of a frame. The profiling lens **measured** removing *every* `clip-path` attribute
in the document at **93% of baseline** (0.93 / 1.09 / 0.90 across three sandwiched
rounds) — indistinguishable from zero. There are only three clipped elements in the file,
and Chrome converts a clip to a mask once and reuses it. **Leave them alone.** This
substantially weakens the case for the build-time polygon boolean *as a performance
measure* — it remains a real payload item (~390–467 KB raw, ~115 KB gzipped, ~11% of all
vertices, and it would end the split stroke), but it should no longer be sold as a way to
make panning faster, and it carries the risk that a wrong boolean is a visible
cartographic error rather than a slow frame.

**Viewport-based unloading does not pay.** Bytes by longitude of subpath centre: 60–100°E
21.4%, **100–130°E 69.4%**, 130–150°E 5.3%, **150°E–210°E just 3.9%**. A view of India
still intersects 24.9% of the file's bytes; the whole Pacific east of 150°E is 5.3% of
bytes and 5.5% of vertices. To save anything meaningful the scheme would have to unload
China, Indochina and India — the atoms the entire hover, selection and admin-graft
machinery is built around — and browsers already cull offscreen paths, so the raster
saving would be small in any case.

Also checked and empty: zero subpaths lie fully outside the viewBox; zero rings have a
bbox span below 0.021 units; there are no `<text>` elements in the SVG at all (labels are
built by JS); the 255 `islet` + 255 identical transparent `islet-hit` circles are ~9 KB;
`#gaz display:none` changed a frame from 53.17 to 53.04 ms and `.atom-hit display:none`
from 53.17 to 51.5 — hidden and `pointer-events:none` subtrees are properly skipped;
hiding `#labels` changed raster by 0%; hatch patterns flattened to solid fill measured
102%. `placeLabels` is 0.13 ms per step and `syncFine` 0.05 ms over 40 wheel ticks.

## A finding that lands outside performance

**`MAX_ZOOM` is 100 (`map.js:1221`), not 40.** The quality standard this project adopted
last week — "half a pixel at the deepest zoom = 0.021 map units" — is the figure for 40×.
At 100× half a device pixel on a 2,800-device-pixel viewport is about 0.005 units, and
the base geometry is written at one decimal place, a 0.1-unit grid. The fine-coastline
layer covers the 24 windows in the `data-fine` metadata, all of them islands: **mainland
China, India and Indochina have no fine window**, so their base geometry is what a reader
sees at 100×.

This is not a performance question and nothing in this review depends on it, but it is
the same number the India tracing was just held to, and it is worth resolving before that
figure is quoted again. Two coherent answers exist — lower `MAX_ZOOM` to the depth the
geometry actually supports, or extend the fine-window mechanism to the mainlands — and
they are different amounts of work.

The corollary is that **blanket Douglas–Peucker at 0.021 is not the free win it looks
like** (it would drop 35,047 vertices, 16.3%), for that reason and for a second one the
build records at `build_map.py:3105-3118`: two shapes cut from the same coastline and
deduped independently part company by up to `eps` and show as flecks. That is why
`FINE_PRECISION`'s eps is 0.002. A blanket tolerance reopens that between every backing
and its sub-units, between `clip-china` and the china backing, and between the leaseholds
and Liaoning. The fix for that is shared arcs (3.1), not a bigger tolerance.

## Reflection: what this adds up to

The distribution of cost is lopsided in a way that is genuinely useful. Nine of the
things one would naturally suspect — clips, patterns, overlays, node count, the label
engine, the gazetteer, the inline SVG at rest, offscreen fine layers, `non-scaling-stroke`
at normal zoom — measured at zero. Two things account for essentially everything. That
means there is no long tail to grind through: there are two decisions and a handful of
cheap tidy-ups.

**The two decisions are both about whether a gesture may look different while it is
happening.** A composited pan is pixel-identical and should not be controversial. A
composited *zoom* is soft until it settles. A thinner stroke is a permanently different
map. A mitred join is, on this geometry, almost certainly not — and it is the single best
ratio in the whole review: one CSS line, a quarter to a third of raster, measured three
times.

**The cheap tidy-ups are worth more than they look on desktop**, because they are
main-thread work and the phone's main thread is four times slower: the per-frame
`defaultView()`, the 1,641 transform writes to elements 40% of which are invisible, the
dead `#browse` layer, the tooltip's forced layout, the unguarded `applyGazetteer`.
Together roughly 7 ms/frame desktop and 28 ms/frame on a throttled phone, all of it
invisible. Mobile's wheel path — 80–176 ms per step against 9–43 on desktop — is
*entirely* this class of work.

**The payload items and the frame-rate items are largely disjoint**, and conflating them
has already produced one wrong estimate in this review. The relative-coordinate encoding
saves 1.8 MB and zero raster. The clips cost 313 KB and zero raster. The seams cost
318 KB and 2% of a frame. Conversely the mitred join saves zero bytes and a third of the
frame. Both axes are worth improving; they should be argued for separately, and the
payload ones sold as load time and memory, which is what they are.

**One caveat about all of it.** These numbers come from headless Chrome with software
rasterisation, on a machine running three benchmarking agents at once — and the
profiling agent's own CPU canary drifted 3× within single runs. Every ratio here was
taken from interleaved or sandwiched A/B pairs for that reason, and the ones that matter
reproduced across agents that never saw each other's work: the viewBox multiplier
(198→1.5 ms one way, 21.3→2.1 tiles the other), the stroke share (85% and 87%), the
mitred join (62% and 74%). The absolute frame times should not be quoted to anyone. A
real device with GPU rasterisation may distribute the work differently, though stroke
tessellation is CPU work on every platform, and the mobile emulation throttles only the
renderer main thread — a real phone throttles its raster workers too, which would make
raster *more* dominant there, not less.

**If any of this is acted on, the order that gets the most for the least risk** is: the
mitred join and the invisible main-thread tidy-ups first, because they are cheap,
measurable and change nothing; then the composited pan, which is the single largest
change available and is pixel-identical for pan alone; then `non-scaling-stroke` →
scaled `stroke-width`, which is what fixes deep zoom and the wheel path the composited
pan leaves behind; then the payload items as a separate exercise. The stroke width and
the shared-arc rebuild are the two that deserve their own conversation before any code is
written — the first because it changes the map, the second because it is the only thing
here that would let both the backings *and* the stroke go, and it is a real project.
