# The trains that move too fast

*An investigation, 4 September 2026. Nothing in the map or its data was changed
by it.*

Watching the train tools, some trains cross the country faster than any 1930s
train could. This asks how many, how fast, and — for each one — whether it is a
fast train, a misread clock, or something else entirely.

**The short answer: almost none of it is a misread clock.** Of 12,263 timed legs
across both timetables, 138 imply an impossible speed, and they fall into three
causes, none of which is the transcription of a time. Two of the three can be
fixed without going back to the printed page at all.

---

## How it was measured

For every train, every pair of consecutive calls that carries a clock at both
ends, two distances:

* **as drawn** — the sum of the traced track between each pair of stations the
  train's stop list passes through, which is the line the animation actually
  moves the mark along;
* **as the crow flies** — the great-circle distance between the two calls. This
  is a *hard floor*: no route between two points is shorter than the straight
  line, so a speed computed from it cannot be an artefact of the drawing.

A leg is only called impossible if it breaks the ceiling on the second figure.

| | timed legs | median | 90th | 99th | fastest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Taiwan 1936 | 3,192 | 35.4 km/h | 49.0 | 115.9 | 1,171.8 |
| Korea 1938 | 9,071 | 34.0 km/h | 50.5 | 1,858.0 | 3,716.1 |

The medians are exactly what a 1930s stopping train looks like. The tails are
not speeds at all.

*Legs whose stations have no coordinate — the bus stops and the halts on the
private narrow-gauge lines — are left out: 1,040 in Taiwan and 3,127 in Korea.
They draw nothing, so they cannot look fast.*

## What a train of the 1930s could actually do

The ceilings below are deliberately generous — set above anything a scheduled
train of the period sustained, so that crossing one is evidence of a fault
rather than of an unusually good run. They are from the general record rather
than from a source in this repository, and should be treated as orders of
magnitude:

* **Taiwan** — 1,067 mm narrow gauge. The island's expresses averaged around
  45–50 km/h and their maxima were of the order of 65–75 km/h. **Ceiling used:
  80 km/h.**
* **Korea** — standard gauge, 1,435 mm, and much faster ground. The 1936
  *Akatsuki* between Fuzan and Keijō was booked at about 6¾ hours for some
  450 km, near 67 km/h average, and was the fastest scheduled train in the
  colony. **Ceiling used: 110 km/h.**
* For scale, the fastest scheduled train anywhere in the empire was the South
  Manchuria Railway's *Asia*, from 1934: about 110 km/h maximum and 82.5 km/h
  average, and that was neither of these railways.

What the data itself says, once the faults below are set aside, agrees. The
fastest end-to-end runs in either sheet, measured on straight-line distance and
so an understatement of perhaps a tenth:

    Korea 1938   train 17/18   Gyeongbu Line    351.9 km in 397 min   53.2 km/h
    Korea 1938   train 8       Gyeongui Line    346.4 km in 406 min   51.2 km/h
    Korea 1938   train 7       Gyeongui Line    346.4 km in 421 min   49.4 km/h
    Taiwan 1936  train 1       Trunk Line       171.8 km in 223 min   46.2 km/h

Add the tenth back and train 17 is doing about 58 km/h over the ground on the
Fuzan–Keijō trunk. That is a plausible express of 1938 and it is the fastest
thing in either timetable. **No genuinely fast train in this data is anywhere
near the ceiling.**

## The three causes

| | Taiwan | Korea | trains touched |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** a call timed on another line | 21 legs | 1 leg | 10 |
| **B** impossible even in a straight line | 2 legs | 117 legs | 63 |
| **C** only the drawn track is too long | 16 legs | 4 legs | 20 |

87 of 1,188 trains — 7.3% — have at least one leg affected: 24 of Taiwan's 342
and 63 of Korea's 846.

### A — the connection row is being flown as a leg

**This is the whole of Taiwan's problem, and it is already flagged in the data.**

The printed tables put a neighbouring line's times in the same column so a
reader can see the connection. The transcription marks those rows — flag `1`,
"timed on another line" — and the build carries the flag through. The animation
does not act on it, so the train is drawn calling there.

Taiwan train 43 is the clearest case. Its stop list runs down the coast:

    沙鹿  dep 20:23
    臺中  dep 20:30   flag 1
    龍井  dep 20:31
    大肚  dep 20:38
    追分  dep 20:49
    彰化  arr 20:58  dep 21:05
    臺中  arr 21:15   flag 1
    花壇  dep 21:18

Taichū appears twice, both times flagged. The train is a coast-line service; it
never went there. As drawn it leaves Shalu, jumps 17 km inland to Taichung in
six minutes (171 km/h), jumps back to Longjing in one (954 km/h), runs down the
coast to Changhua, goes inland to Taichung again, and comes back to Huatan.

    彰化 -> 臺中   16.1 km in  5 min = 193 km/h   x4 trains
    花壇 -> 臺中   19.5 km in  2 min = 586 km/h   x3
    臺中 -> 龍井   15.9 km in  1 min = 954 km/h   x3
    沙鹿 -> 臺中   17.1 km in  6 min = 171 km/h   x2
    大甲 -> 臺中   23.8 km in 14 min = 102 km/h   x1

Korea has one of these: train 505's Seishin–Keijō row, 525 km in 258 minutes
(122 km/h), which is a through-connection note and not a leg of that train.

**The times are right. The route is wrong, and the flag to fix it is already in
the file.** Skipping flag-1 rows when the running line is built would take all
22 legs out and would also stop the visible zig-zag, which is the same fault
seen rather than measured.

### B — one station standing in for another

**This is the whole of Korea's problem: 117 legs, 26 station records, one cause.**

The Korea build matches the timetable's station names to `kr-stations.js` by
name, folded through a table of 舊字體 variants. Korean station names repeat
across the peninsula, and several matched the wrong one. The station then sits
hundreds of kilometres from its neighbours and every train through it flies.

The decisive case leaves no room for doubt:

    鏡城  Kyōjō   at 126.971, 37.555 — which is *exactly* 京城 Keijō's coordinate

鏡城 is in North Hamgyŏng, near Chŏngjin. 京城 is Seoul. In hangul both are
경성, and in McCune–Reischauer both are Kyŏngsŏng. The Hamgyŏng station has been
given Seoul's position, 743 km away, and 30 legs fly the gap — train 508 does
Ranam to Kyŏngsŏng, twelve minutes, 3,716 km/h.

The others are the same kind of collision:

    松亭  Shōtei   34 legs   placed at 129.202, 35.183 — the Busan 송정, 4.4 km
                             from Haeundae, yet the record is on both the East
                             Sea Line and the Chungbuk Line, 300 km apart
    龍門  Ryūmon   24 legs   placed at 127.594, 37.482 — east of Seoul, but the
                             record belongs to the Hwanghae Line in the west;
                             its nearest neighbour on its own line is 112.8 km off
    麻田  Maden    20 legs   at 37.03 N among neighbours at 40.0 N
    丁峰  Teihō    17 legs   Chungbuk Line
    淸州  Shōshū   17 legs   Chungbuk Line
    羅南  Ranan    15 legs   drags in 鏡城 above

A quick and reliable test for the rest: for each station, the distance to the
nearest other station on its own line. Every one of the suspects except the
homonym pairs sits within a few kilometres of a neighbour, so a threshold of
about 20 km would name the misplacements and nothing else. 龍門's 112.8 km is
the extreme.

Taiwan has two in this class and both are small:

    train 113  Yilan Line   宜蘭 -> 二結   5.6 km in 2 min = 168 km/h
    train 304  Trunk Line   八堵 -> 基隆   2.8 km in 2 min =  85 km/h

**These two are the only candidates in either sheet for a misread time**, and
even they are more likely a two-minute booking over a short distance than a
digit gone wrong: Badu to Keelung is a few minutes' run in any timetable. They
are worth a look at the printed page and nothing more.

### C — the drawn track wanders

Twenty legs where the straight-line speed is fine and only the traced path is
too long: the geometry between two stations follows something other than the
railway. Two of these are consequences of B — a path traced to a station that
is in the wrong place:

    Korea   前津 -> 新北青    track 1131.0 km   straight 36.7 km
    Korea   淸州 -> 丁峰      track  710.3 km   straight  8.7 km

The rest are ordinary tracing detours, small and harmless to the clock but
visible as a mark taking a scenic route:

    Taiwan  豐田 -> 壽        track  11.6 km   straight  2.7 km   x10 legs
    Taiwan  彰化 -> 追分       track  30.4 km   straight  5.3 km   x4
    Taiwan  追分 -> 臺中       track  44.0 km   straight 11.8 km   x1
    Korea   上仁川 -> 仁川      track   4.0 km   straight  1.1 km
    Korea   新光州 -> 全南光州    track   7.6 km   straight  2.8 km

Across all 1,064 traced station pairs only six exceed two and a half times their
straight line, so this is a short list rather than a systemic problem.

## What I would do about it

In the order the effort pays:

1. **Skip the flag-1 rows when the running line is built.** One condition. It
   removes every one of Taiwan's 21 impossible legs and the visible zig-zag
   through Taichung, and it uses a flag the data already carries. Nothing is
   lost: the connection times still belong in the card, which is where a reader
   reads them.
2. **Add a build-time guard on station placement.** For every station, the
   distance to the nearest other station on the same line; refuse, or at least
   report, anything beyond about 20 km. That names all 26 Korean suspects — it
   would have caught 鏡城 sitting on 京城's coordinate before the file shipped —
   and it costs one pass over the table.
3. **Disambiguate the homonyms by line, not by name alone.** The Korea build
   matches on the folded name; the timetable also knows which line the station
   is on, and no two of these collisions share a line. 松亭 carrying two line
   ids is the tell: one record is standing in for two stations 300 km apart.
4. **Re-trace the six long paths** listed under C. Small, cosmetic, and
   independent of everything above.
5. **Look at the two Taiwan clocks** — 宜蘭→二結 on train 113 and 八堵→基隆 on
   train 304 — against the printed page. This is the only part of the exercise
   that needs the source, and it concerns two legs out of 12,263.

A speed guard in the test suite would keep all of it from coming back: assert
that no leg in either timetable exceeds its sheet's ceiling on the straight-line
distance, excluding flag-1 rows. It runs on the data alone, needs no browser,
and it is the measurement above, kept.

---

## In one line

There is no evidence of OCR trouble with the times. What looks like a train
doing 3,700 km/h is a station in the wrong place, and what looks like one doing
190 km/h in Taiwan is a connection row being flown as though the train called
there. Both are fixable in the build, and one of them with a flag the file
already carries.
