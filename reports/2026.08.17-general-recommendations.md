# The Japanese Empire map as a teaching tool

**A pedagogical audit — coverage, framing, assessment, scaffolding, and the pairing with the China map**
17 August 2026

---

## What this is, and what I read

An assessment of the application *as a thing students learn from*, not as cartography. I read
`README.md`, `index.html`, `data.js` (2,446 lines), `map.js` (3,635 lines), `SOURCES.md`,
`tasks.md` and `tasks-done.md` end to end, the 11 August `recommendations.md`, the companion map
in `inspiration/china-map/`, and — this turns out to matter more than anything else — **the three
module timelines in `inspiration/`**, which `SOURCES.md` names as the authority for the map's
place list. Numbers below were computed from the files, not estimated.

Nothing in the project was modified.

---

## 0. The verdict in one paragraph

This is a superb historical *gazetteer* and an unfinished *teaching instrument*. The cartography
has reached a standard that university teaching maps essentially never reach: 129 territories
composed per epoch from a shared atom set, 477 named period sub-units, 890 gazetteer entries, two
independent sources for Chinese provincial boundaries with the difference measured, a traced
occupation zone with 75 Communist base areas hatched over it, and an open-issues list down to
three stray pixels in the Kwantung leasehold. Against that, the teaching layer has one exercise
(click the named place), one path through the material (none), two dates, five event markers
before 1931, and no quiz at all on a phone. The imbalance is visible in the repository itself: of
2,300 lines in `tasks.md`, roughly 120 bear on pedagogy, and the entire open queue is two
sub-pixel geometry questions. **The next order of magnitude of teaching value is not in the
geometry. It is in what the application asks students to do with it, and in the twenty per cent of
the content that carries the empire's human history and is currently unreachable unless you happen
to click the right city.**

If only five things are done, do these:

1. **Put events on the 1930 map.** At Basic and Intermediate it has *zero*. The Events switch is a
   dead control across half the application. Data-only.
2. **Take the nine post-1942 battles off the Dec 1942 map**, or add the epoch they belong to.
   Saipan (1944) is `lvl: 1`, so a Basic student on the December 1942 map is asked to find a
   battle eighteen months in its future. One line of `map.js`, or a new epoch.
3. **A short session and a "practise the ten I missed" button**, with each missed place's `note`
   and date in the summary. Asked for on 11 August; still open; still the highest-value small
   change in the application.
4. **Tag the existing notes with themes and let students browse by theme.** Everything about
   forced labour, massacre, settlement, collaboration and famine is already written and is
   currently discoverable only by clicking the one right place. This costs no new prose.
5. **A printable blank map**, because the module's assessment is a paper labelling test and the
   China half of the module already has one (`inspiration/china-map/chinamap.pdf`). Nothing in
   `tasks.md` has ever mentioned printing.

## 2. Coverage

### 2.1 The map covers a third of the module's timeline

`SOURCES.md` states the rule: "Every city, battle and incident on the map corresponds to an event
in those timelines." The timelines are in the repository. Here is the main one,
`01modernjapan.txt`, "Modern Japan from 1850–1989", 243 dated entries, against what the map
offers:

| Period | Timeline entries | Event markers on the map |
|---|---|---|
| 1853–1894 | 56 | 2 (Uraga 1853, Kanghwa 1876) |
| 1895–1910 | 23 | 1 (Tsushima 1905) |
| 1911–1930 | 36 | 1 (Nikolaevsk 1920) |
| 1931–1936 | 21 | 1 (Wanpaoshan 1931) |
| 1937–1941 | 17 | 6 |
| 1942 | 4 | 8 |
| 1943–1945 | 14 | 9 |
| 1946–1952 | 27 | 0 |
| 1953–1989 | 45 | 0 |

Read the two ends of that table. **115 timeline entries — 47% of the module's dated chronology —
fall in the period the 1930 sheet represents, and the 1930 sheet carries five event markers, all
of them at Advanced.** A further **72 entries (30%) fall after the map's end date entirely**, and
the module's third timeline runs explicitly to 1952. The map is aligned to the war years, which
are 18 of 243 entries.

This is not an argument that the map should become a timeline. It is an argument that the map's own
stated sourcing rule has been applied unevenly, and that the unevenness happens to strip out the
half of the module where the empire is *being made* and the half where it is *being dismantled* —
which is to say, both halves where causation is taught.

### 2.2 The 1930 map is not finished

Measured pool sizes:

| | territories | cities | events | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1930 Basic** | 12 | 11 | **0** | 23 |
| **1930 Intermediate** | 22 | 33 | **0** | 55 |
| 1930 Advanced | 63 | 44 | 5 | 112 |
| 1942 Basic | 14 | 11 | 3 | 28 |
| 1942 Intermediate | 29 | 34 | 9 | 72 |
| 1942 Advanced | 66 | 50 | 28 | 144 |

A student at Basic — the default, and where most will stay — switches on Events on the 1930 map
and *nothing appears*. Not "few things": nothing. The five pre-1931 events are all `lvl: 3`.

The whole of Perry, the unequal treaties, the Boshin War, the Satsuma Rebellion, the Taiwan
expedition, the Sino-Japanese War, the Russo-Japanese War, the annexation of Korea, the seizure of
Tsingtao, the Twenty-One Demands, the Siberian intervention, March First, May Fourth, the Kantō
earthquake, Jinan, Huanggutun and the Mukden Incident itself is represented, on the map dated
1930, by five dots at the highest difficulty setting.

Some of that history *is* in the file — as `date` strings on `cat: 'city'` records, roughly twenty
of them: the Battle of Pyongyang (15 September 1894), the siege of Port Arthur, Qingdao seized
from Germany (November 1914), the Jinan Incident (May 1928), the Manchurian Incident (18 September
1931), and so on. But because they sit on city records they are invisible when Events is off,
they are never asked about as events, and they cannot be found by a student looking for what
happened. The content exists and the structure hides it.

**Named additions for the 1930 sheet**, all timeline-anchored, all data-only. Where a city record
already exists the work is an `EPOCH_OVERRIDES` note, not a new marker:

*New event markers.* Namamugi (Richardson Affair, 14 September 1862); Kagoshima bombardment
(15–17 August 1863) — the city exists, the event does not; Shimonoseki bombardment (September
1864); Toba–Fushimi and Goryōkaku (Boshin War, 1868–69); Shiroyama, Kagoshima (Satsuma Rebellion,
September 1877); the Mudan expedition, southern Taiwan (May–December 1874) — the map's first
overseas use of force and currently absent; Asan/Pungdo (25 July 1894, the first shots of the
Sino-Japanese War); **Liutiaogou, north of Mukden (18 September 1931)** — the single most
consequential location in the module and not a marker; **Huanggutun (4 June 1928)**, three
kilometres from it; **Musha/Wushe, Taiwan (27 October 1930)** — contemporaneous with the map's own
date, and the obvious way to put the violence of Taiwanese colonial rule onto a map that currently
does not have it; **Kwangju (November 1929)**; Suwon/Cheam-ni (April 1919), for what "met with
mass repression" actually meant.

*Promote to `lvl` 1 or 2* so the Events switch works at Basic: Uraga, Kanghwa, Tsushima, and
whichever of the above are added. Three events at Basic on the 1930 sheet would be enough to make
the control honest.

*Existing cities needing their 1930 event in the note:* Shimonoseki (the treaty of 17 April 1895 —
the note currently does not mention it), Qingdao (the November 1914 siege, Japan's entry into the
war), Port Arthur (the 1894 massacre as well as the 1904–05 siege), Weihaiwei (February 1895),
Vladivostok (the August 1918 landing).

### 2.3 The Dec 1942 map is doing the work of three dates

`siteInEpoch()` filters by year for 1930 only (`map.js:200–203`). So the December 1942 map carries
Tarawa (November 1943), Kwajalein, Truk, Peleliu, Tinian, Saipan, Imphal, Leyte Gulf (all 1944)
and Iwo Jima (1945). Nine markers, one of them at Basic.

This was flagged on 11 August and is still open. I raise it again because it is not merely
untidy — it teaches a false chronology to exactly the students least able to catch it, and it
does so on the sheet whose entire pedagogical purpose is *this is what the empire looked like at
this moment*. The 1930 sheet is disciplined about this; the 1942 sheet is not, and the
inconsistency is the error.

There are only two honest fixes and they point in different directions. Make the filter symmetric
and lose nine module events from the application; or admit that the corpus already contains a
1944–45 epoch's worth of content and give it the epoch. I recommend the second (§2.4).

### 2.4 Are 1930 and December 1942 the right two dates, and what a third should be

The pair is well chosen and the reason is good: 1930 asks "whose empire is this?" and 1942 asks
"how did Japan hold what it took?", and opening on 1930 so the student sees the empire small
before seeing it large is exactly right (`data.js:50–53`).

What two snapshots cost is **process**. Every mechanism the module actually examines is a
transition, and a transition is precisely what two static sheets cannot show: the 1895–1910
acquisition sequence, the 1931–33 salami tactics, the 1937–41 escalation, the 1944–45 collapse,
the 1945–52 dismantling. A student can see that Korea was Chinese-adjacent in 1930 and Japanese in
1942 — but Korea was Japanese in both. The dates were chosen so that the *war* is a change, and
everything else is background.

Do not fix this with a time slider. The sources cannot support one, interpolation between traced
period sheets would manufacture boundaries nobody drew, and it would violate the project's own
epistemics ("the rendered colour is the honest test"). Fix it with more discrete, sourced epochs,
in this order of cost-to-value:

**(a) August 1945 — cheapest and most valuable.** All nine orphaned battle markers land here.
It brings in Okinawa (April–June 1945, already a `date` on the Naha record), the firebombing of
Tokyo, Hiroshima and Nagasaki (already in the data, correctly suppressed in 1930), the Soviet
invasion of Manchuria, and the Potsdam/surrender geography. Geometry needed: almost none — the
territorial extent shrinks from shapes already held, the atoms all exist. This is the epoch the
data is already half-built for.

**(b) 1952 — the module's own endpoint, and the biggest single teaching gain.** The third module
timeline ends at "End of allied occupation of Japan, 1952". A 1952 sheet answers the question the
present map cannot ask: *what happened to all of this?* Japan reduced to four islands under
American occupation; Okinawa and the Ogasawaras retained by the United States until 1972 and 1968;
Korea divided at the 38th parallel and at war; two Chinas; independent Philippines (1946), India
and Pakistan (1947), Burma (1948), Indonesia (1949); French Indochina at war; Malaya in the
Emergency; the Nan'yō a US Trust Territory. Geometry needed: the 38th parallel (a parallel, so as
cheap as Karafuto's 50th), the 17th parallel, and successor-state outlines that are all modern and
therefore already in the atom set. **Decolonisation is the one large gap that no file in the
repository has ever acknowledged** — zero mentions across `tasks.md`, `README.md`, `SOURCES.md`
and `recommendations.md` — and it is where the module spends its last third.

**(c) 1910 — the most desirable and the most expensive.** It would show the empire the module's
first half builds: Taiwan 1895, Karafuto 1905, the Kwantung lease, Korea annexed that August. The
blocker is China: the ENP provinces are 1928–45, so a 1910 sheet needs Qing-era provincial
geography, which is real cartographic work. Worth planning, not worth doing before (a) and (b).

### 2.5 Is the regional treatment proportionate?

Event and city markers by region (78 sites):

| Region | Sites |
|---|---|
| China proper | 19 |
| Japan home islands | 17 |
| Pacific / Micronesia | 16 |
| Southeast Asia | 12 |
| Korea | 5 |
| Manchuria | 8 |
| Taiwan | **2** |
| Karafuto / Kurils | **0** |
| India / Burma | 2 |

**Taiwan and Karafuto are the failures.** Taiwan has two cities (Taihoku, Takao), no events, and
notes that are purely acquisitional — "Japan's first modern colony, ceded by the Qing in the 1895
Treaty of Shimonoseki" in 1930, and in 1942 "the oldest colony and the staging ground for the
invasion of the Philippines". Nothing on the 1895–1902 pacification war, the campaigns against the
highland peoples, Musha, *kōminka*, the sugar monopoly, or the roughly 207,000 Taiwanese who
served in Japanese uniform. Korea's 1942 note, by contrast, does the work in one sentence:
"Korean-language teaching suppressed, Japanese names imposed from 1940, and mobilisation of
labour and, from 1944, conscription." **The two oldest colonies are weighted very differently for
no stated reason**, and a student comparing them will conclude that Taiwan's colonial experience
was thinner. That conclusion is wrong and the map is the source of it.

Karafuto has **zero sites**. Toyohara and Ōtomari exist only as unremarked gazetteer dots. A
colony held for forty years, with an indigenous population (Ainu, Nivkh, Uilta), a large Korean
conscript-labour force, and a violent 1945 ending, has no clickable content whatever beyond its
territory record.

Micronesia, by contrast, is well served — sixteen sites, the Nan'yō-chō at Koror named, and the
single best settler-demography sentence in the file: "Japanese settlers came with the sugar
industry on Saipan and Tinian and outnumbered the islanders by the mid-1930s." That sentence is
the register the colonies should all be written in.

India is nearly absent as *places*: no Indian city is a site at all, only Rangoon and
Imphal–Kohima. Given the Andamans, the INA, Quit India and the Bengal famine of 1943 are all
module-adjacent, one or two Indian sites would earn their place.

### 2.6 The ratio that says it all

The application contains roughly **1,470 named places** (890 gazetteer + 170 browse + 477
sub-units, plus 129 territories) and **28 events**. It knows where everything was and very little
about what happened. For a geography-revision aid that is the right ratio. For a history module it
is the wrong one, and the correction is cheap because it is prose, not geometry.

## 3. Framing: whose empire, and whose experience?

### 3.1 The ontology is the polity

There are exactly two kinds of thing in `data.js`: a `territory`, whose defining attribute is
`cat` — the power that ruled it — and a `site`, which is `city` or `battle`. There is no category
for a people, a movement, a flow, a policy or a number. This is not a stylistic observation; it
determines what the application can ask. Every question derivable from the schema is *where is X*
or *whose was X*. No question about who lived there, what was taken from it, who resisted, or how
long it had been held is expressible, because those facts are not fields — they are sentences
inside `note`.

So: is it a map of flags and boundaries? **Less than it looks, and more than it should be.** The
notes are considerably better than the schema. They carry Nanjing ("weeks of mass killing and
rape"), Sook Ching, Unit 731 at Pingfang, the Kantō earthquake massacre of Koreans, the Tokyo
firebombing with a figure, the Tonkin famine of 1944–45 with a figure and a cause, the Timorese
death toll, Hainan "worked with forced labour for its iron ore", Marpi Point, and — genuinely
well done — collaboration handled as politics rather than as treachery: Sukarno and Hatta, Ba Maw,
José Laurel, Wang Ching-wei, Bose and the renaming of the Andamans as Shaheed and Swaraj.

But all of it is *inside place records*, one clause deep, reachable only by clicking the one place
that happens to carry it. A student revising for a map quiz will click Korea and Taiwan and
Manchukuo and Singapore, and will never click Hainan, Harbin, Sapporo, Kobe or Portuguese Timor,
which is where four of the map's strongest sentences live. **The empire's human history is present
in the data and absent from the experience.** That is the most correctable framing problem in the
project, and it needs no new content (§7, item D1).

### 3.2 What colouring by ruling power does

On the 1930 sheet it is exactly right and it is the sheet's whole argument: thirteen colours, one
per empire, and the point lands in three seconds.

On the 1942 sheet the four-step salmon ramp — metropole → colony → client state → military
occupation — is analytically excellent. It encodes *mode of rule*, which is a real and teachable
distinction, and it can be read by shade alone.

It has two side effects worth naming.

**Duration is invisible.** Twenty-two of the 66 territories on the 1942 sheet are `occupied`, one
flat colour. Korea after thirty-two years and Java after nine months are adjacent shades on one
ramp. A student looking at the December 1942 map sees a single coherent empire; what they should
see is a forty-seven-year-old colonial core with a twelve-month-old military conquest wrapped
round it. This is one of the most important things about the December 1942 map and the colouring
actively conceals it. A cheap fix: a `since` year on each 1942 territory (derivable from the
`when` strings already there) and an alternative shading — "how long had this been Japanese?" —
banded 1895–1910 / 1910–1932 / 1932–1941 / 1941–1942. Three or four hours of work, and it converts
the map's headline claim from a static fact into a chronology.

**Population is invisible.** There are no numbers anywhere in the application. "How big was the
Japanese empire" is answered only in area — and answered wrongly, because Mercator exaggerates
×1.70 at 40°N and ×2.42 at 50°N, so Attu is drawn 2.7 times too large relative to Java (measured
in the 11 August review). Roughly thirty numbers in `data.js` — Japan proper 73m, Korea 24m,
Taiwan 5.9m, Manchukuo 43m, Java 48m, the Philippines 16m, Malaya 5.5m, Burma 16m — drawn as
proportional circles would answer the question in the terms the module actually cares about, and
would show that the demographic centre of the 1942 empire was Java and north China, not the
Pacific the markers are massed over. Myers and Peattie's statistical appendices and the NDL
Government-General yearbooks are already named in `SOURCES.md`'s reading.

### 3.3 The named content gaps

Twelve of twenty-eight themes I queried are entirely absent from `data.js`. The ones that matter
for this subject, in order of how badly they are missed:

**Comfort women — zero mentions.** No hits on `comfort`, `ianfu`, `慰安`. The module's own main
timeline carries "First Japanese newspaper article on 'comfort women', 1984", so this is in scope
by the map's own sourcing rule, and its absence from a map of this empire in 2026 will be noticed
by students before it is noticed by anyone else. The minimum is three clauses and a theme tag:
Korea (where the majority of the women came from), Shanghai (the first military comfort station,
1932), and the system's expansion after December 1937. Yoshimi is already in the reading list.

**Forced and conscripted labour — two clauses in the whole file.** Hainan's "worked with forced
labour for its iron ore" and Korea's "mobilisation of labour and, from 1944, conscription". Absent:
the Javanese *rōmusha* levies, Korean and Chinese conscript labour inside Japan, and the POW/labour
complex as a system.

**The Burma–Siam railway — absent, and the map already has the place.**
`data.js:2377` reads `'Kanchanaburi': { en: 'Kanchanaburi (Kanburi)' }` — a bare changwat name with
no note. The railway was under construction *at the map's own date*: begun June 1942, completed
October 1943, roughly 60,000 Allied prisoners and more than 200,000 Asian labourers, with
mortality an order of magnitude worse among the latter. Two markers — Thanbyuzayat and Ban
Pong/Nong Pladuk — with the line between them, is a half-hour of data entry and is the single most
teachable omission in the application.

**Opium — zero mentions.** The monopolies in Manchukuo and occupied China were an instrument of
rule and a source of revenue, and Manchukuo's note (P'u-i, Jehol, the Kwantung Army) is exactly
where the sentence goes.

**Taiwan's indigenous peoples — zero mentions**, against two for the Ainu. See §2.5.

**Resistance outside China — thin.** The Communist base areas are a drawn layer, which is
excellent; Nationalist guerrilla zones are named in `README.md` and drawn nowhere; the Hukbalahap
appears only as "a large guerrilla resistance in the hills"; the Viet Minh, the MPAJA and Seri
Thai are absent by name. As it stands the map teaches that Chinese resistance was territorial and
Southeast Asian resistance was atmospheric.

**Migration and settlement — one good sentence and no subject.** Present: Hokkaidō "colonised from
1869 at the expense of the Ainu", the Nan'yō settler majority, Kobe as "the main emigration port
for Japanese leaving for Hawaii and the Americas", Hawaii's "large Japanese immigrant population",
Xiamen as a departure point for Chinese emigration. Absent: the *manmō kaitakudan* settler
programme in Manchukuo (the single largest state-sponsored settlement scheme in the empire),
Korean labour migration into Osaka and the Kyūshū coalfields — the population that the 1923
massacre, which *is* covered, was drawn from — Davao's abaca colony, and the Americas beyond one
clause. Brazil and Peru are outside the 66°E–206°E frame and always will be, so the diaspora needs
either an off-frame annotation or to be carried in the notes of the ports people left from.

**After the surrender — nothing at all.** No repatriation (`hikiage`: zero hits, some 6.5 million
people), no war-crimes trials, no decolonisation. This is the same gap as §2.4(b), seen from the
content side.

### 3.4 A note on register

Where the notes engage violence they are good, plain and unflinching, and they use figures. Two
places where the register slips: Unit 731's "biological warfare experiments" is a euphemism for
human vivisection and the field-testing of plague and cholera, and chemical warfare in China is
absent entirely. The Nanjing note names the massacre and gives no figure, where Tokyo, Hiroshima,
Timor and Tonkin all get one; whatever range is chosen, the asymmetry is conspicuous on this
particular event.

## 4. The quiz

### 4.1 Find-the-place is the right *core* exercise, and the reason is in the other repository

`inspiration/china-map/index.html` says what the module actually assesses:

> **Map Quiz:** This was created for use in my own teaching to help students prepare for their map
> quiz. Students will be given a blank map like the one on this page and asked to label the
> provinces, name the cities, and make their best attempt at drawing the (modern) course of the
> Yellow and Yangzi rivers.

So find-the-place is not an arbitrary choice of exercise — it is a rehearsal of the real
assessment, and criticising it for not testing causation misses the design. Keep it as the core.
The criticism that stands is that it is the *only* thing the application does with a dataset that
can support four more exercises at almost no cost, and that it has no session design.

### 4.2 Four more question types the data already supports

Nothing here needs new content. Everything needed is already a field.

1. **Reverse direction — "what is this?"** The map points at a place; the student names it, or
   picks from four. This is the *other* half of the paper quiz ("name the cities") and the
   application currently rehearses only one half.
2. **Chronology — and it is free.** Every one of the 78 sites carries a numeric `year`. "Which came
   first?" and "put these four in order" need no new data at all. This also produces the first
   exercise in the application that requires no map click, which means it is the first one that
   works with a keyboard, with a screen reader, and on a phone.
3. **Script drill.** Prompt in `zh`, `ja` or `ko`; answer on the map. The four-script dataset is
   the map's most distinctive asset and the quiz makes no use of it. For students reading Japanese
   or Chinese sources this is a genuinely better revision tool than any paper map, and it is the
   one exercise here that a paper map cannot rehearse at all. (Raised on 11 August; still open;
   I would rank it higher than it was ranked there.)
4. **Category — "click a place under military occupation in December 1942."** Uses `cat`, which
   every territory has. The legend currently teaches nothing because nothing ever asks about it;
   this makes the eighteen-row colour legend into content.

And one that needs a small amount of new structure but is the most valuable of all for a history
module:

5. **Then and now, across the two epochs.** "This was Chinese in 1930. What was it in 1942?" The
   application already holds both states of every territory and has no exercise that compares
   them. This is the only question type that assesses *change over time*, which is what the module
   is about, and the two-epoch architecture was built for it.

### 4.3 Session design

`startQuiz` shuffles the whole pool and pops from it, so an Advanced 1942 session is **144
questions**. Nobody finishes that. There is no session length, no "practise what I missed", and the
end-of-quiz "Worth another look" list is inert text — not clickable, no notes, no dates, despite
every record carrying both. A student who missed fourteen places is handed fourteen names and no
help.

The fix is small and was already asked for: sessions of ten or twenty; a "practise these again"
button off the summary; and the missed place's `note` and `date` in the summary itself. That last
one is the thing a paper map physically cannot do, and it is currently left on the table.

Wrong-answer feedback likewise names your error and teaches nothing about the target — "That is
Shimoda — try again." A second attempt could offer the target's date, its ruling power, or a
direction, all of which are already in the record.

### 4.4 The printable blank map

The China map ships `chinamap.pdf`, a blank map for the paper quiz. The Japan map ships nothing,
and **`tasks.md` contains no occurrence of "print", "PDF", "blank map" or "handout"** — this has
never been considered.

This is a straightforward win: a "Print this view" button producing the current view with
coastlines and boundaries only, no names, no fills or a light greyscale, optionally with numbered
call-outs and a separate key. The SVG pipeline already exists; most of the work is print CSS and a
label-suppression pass. It closes the loop between the revision tool and the actual assessment,
and it gives the teacher something to hand out that matches exactly what the students revised on —
which the China half of the module already has and this half does not.

## 5. Interaction and scaffolding

### 5.1 The first thirty seconds

A student who knows nothing opens the map and sees: a coloured world with **no names, no city
dots, no event markers and no administrative divisions** (`map.js:44–45`, all four default to
off), a legend of up to eighteen colour words with no way to connect a row to a shape, and a hint
that fades after nine seconds. The two best pieces of orientation prose in the application — the
epoch blurbs, which are genuinely good — fire only on an epoch button *click*, so a student who
never switches dates never sees either.

I take the stated rationale seriously: identical opening state on a shared machine is right, and
Administrative is more than half the payload. But "a map that arrives with three layers already on
gives them nothing to switch" optimises for the confident student. The cost falls on the one who
does not yet know that this application has layers, and who therefore concludes it is a picture.

A middle position that keeps the rationale intact: show the current epoch's blurb on first load
(it is already written, it is already the right length, and it currently cannot be reached), and
have it name the three switches. That is a few lines, it changes no default, and it costs no
payload. Alternatively, a first-visit-only nudge that turns names on once and says so.

### 5.2 There is no path

This is the largest structural gap in the teaching design and it is not mentioned anywhere in
`tasks.md`, `recommendations.md`, `README.md` or `SOURCES.md`. The application offers free
exploration and a quiz. It offers no sequence, no narrative, no task, and nothing a student can be
*set*.

What it needs is small once built, because it is data: a stepped path is a list of
`{epoch, layers, bbox, place, one paragraph}` — and the application already has every one of those
primitives, since the permalink already encodes epoch, layers, level and bounding box in a
base-36 field. A "Guided" mode with Next/Back and a paragraph per step is perhaps 150 lines of
`map.js` plus prose.

Eight paths that would carry the module:

1. **How Japan acquired an empire, 1874–1910** — Mudan, Kanghwa, Asan, Pyongyang, Shimonoseki,
   Taiwan, Port Arthur, Tsushima, Karafuto, Seoul.
2. **The Manchurian Incident and what followed, 1931–33** — Wanpaoshan, Liutiaogou, Hsinking,
   Jehol, the Tanggu line.
3. **How the war in China spread, 1937–41** — Marco Polo Bridge, Shanghai, Nanjing, Huayuankou,
   Wuhan, Chungking, Yenan.
4. **The southern advance, December 1941 – March 1942** — Pearl Harbor, Kota Bharu, Singapore,
   Manila, Rangoon, Batavia.
5. **The empire at its widest — and why the dashed line is not a border.** The line-of-control
   note is already the best short piece of historiographical writing in the project and nothing
   currently makes a student read it.
6. **Who lived in the empire** — migration, settlement, labour, and the demographic centre of the
   1942 map (pairs with §3.2).
7. **How it ended, 1944–45** — needs the August 1945 epoch.
8. **What was left, 1952** — needs the 1952 epoch.

Paths 1, 3 and 5 are buildable with today's data. That alone would change what a seminar can do
with this thing.

### 5.3 Accessibility

**There is no quiz on a phone.** `applyPhoneLayout()` (`map.js:3277–3296`) forces `state.mode` back
to `explore` below 620px, and `showHint()` does not offer the quiz there. The reason given is
sound — the quiz card, its buttons and its feedback line left the map a strip — but the
consequence is that **the students most likely to revise in ten-minute stretches cannot revise**.
This is not mentioned in `recommendations.md`. The fix is not the card: it is a single-line prompt
bar pinned to the top ("Find: Karafuto · Show me · Skip"), with the feedback replacing the prompt
text for a second. The chronology question type (§4.2.2) would also work on a phone without any
map interaction at all.

**No keyboard route into the map, and therefore no keyboard quiz.** No marker or territory has a
`tabindex`; the global handler offers Escape, `0`, `+`, `-`. A keyboard-only or switch user is
locked out of Quiz mode entirely. The cheapest fix that solves this *and* several other problems
at once is a **searchable, tabbable list of the current pool's places in a side panel**, mirroring
the map: selecting a row selects the place. That gives keyboard users a route in, gives screen
readers a non-visual equivalent for hover information that currently has none, gives the quiz a
non-pointer answer path, and gives every student a way to find a place they cannot see.

**Screen readers get no quiz.** `#q-target` and `#q-feedback` have no `aria-live` and no role, so
the question changes and the correct/wrong verdict appears in silence. `role="application"` on
`#map-container` promises keyboard handling that does not exist. CJK names carry no `lang`
attribute, so 東京・도쿄 are read with the English voice. All still open from 11 August.

**Colour.** The 11 August protanopia table stands. Extending it to deuteranopia, which is the
commoner form, the worst collisions on the 1930 sheet are:

| Pair | ΔE normal | ΔE deuteranopia | ΔE protanopia | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australian `#c9a6b0` / Independent `#8dd3c7` | 39.9 | **7.6** | 12.8 | Australian New Guinea reads as Siam |
| Dutch `#fdb462` / De facto independent `#e7de7e` | 29.9 | **9.2** | 11.3 | the Indies read as Tibet or Mongolia |
| Portuguese `#fccde5` / Independent `#8dd3c7` | 45.7 | **9.6** | 12.0 | Macao and Timor read as Siam |
| Independent `#8dd3c7` / Elsewhere `#ded8cb` | 26.5 | 13.4 | **6.2** | the one uncolonised state reads as unmapped |

(CIELAB ΔE76, Machado severity 1.0. The four pairs above are the worst on the 1930 sheet; the
protanopia column reproduces and extends the 11 August measurement.)

Anything under about 10 is indistinguishable across a map at a glance. The three that involve
`independent` all corrupt the same teaching point — that Siam alone was never colonised — and the
file already carries five hatch patterns, so the machinery to fix this without a fifth shade
exists.

**A small thing worth verifying:** `index.html:25–27` ships the three layer buttons with
`class="on" aria-pressed="true"` while `map.js:44` initialises all three to `false`.
`syncLayerButtons()` corrects this during wiring, but if the SVG fetch is slow or fails the reader
sees three switches that say they are on above a map with nothing on it.

## 6. The companion China map

Right now the pairing is an assertion in two READMEs and a copy of the other map in
`inspiration/`. There is no cross-link, no shared vocabulary, no shared interaction model, and one
substantive contradiction.

**The contradiction.** The China map is a map of *modern* provinces — its own text warns that
"China's provinces, autonomous regions, and directly administered cities have undergone a number
of important changes so please consult other maps for earlier periods, especially before the
1960s." The Japan map's 1930 sheet draws **the actual provinces of the Republican period** —
Jehol, Chahar, Suiyuan, Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Xikang — from a scholarly source, with a
second source available as a switch. So the Japan map is, incidentally, a far better map of
Republican China than the China map is, and the module currently hands students two mutually
inconsistent Chinas without telling them which is which or why.

Four things, in order of value:

1. **Cross-link them, and say what each is for.** One line in each About: "the modern provinces
   are here; the provinces of the Republic, 1928–45, are on the Japan map's 1930 sheet." Nearly
   free, and it converts the contradiction into a teaching point about how administrative
   geography changes.
2. **Offer the Japan map's 1930 sheet to the China half of the module** as its Republican-period
   map, via a permalink with the layers preset. The permalink machinery already does this.
3. **One printable pack for both** (§4.4), so the two paper quizzes look like siblings.
4. **Port this engine to the China data.** The China map is an older, simpler codebase — mouseover
   labels, three checkboxes, no quiz, no levels, no scripts. The Japan map's engine — epochs,
   levels, quiz, permalinks, gazetteer, multi-script names — would give the module one interaction
   model across both maps, and China's data (provinces, capitals, rivers) is a small fraction of
   what `data.js` already carries. This is the largest job in this report and probably the largest
   single gain in student experience per unit of new thinking, because everything is already
   designed.

## 8. What I am deliberately not recommending

- **A time slider or continuous timeline.** The sources cannot support it; interpolating between
  traced period sheets would invent boundaries nobody drew; and it contradicts the project's own
  evidentiary standard. More discrete, sourced epochs is the right answer and it is a different
  answer.
- **A language switch.** Removed on purpose; showing every script a place answers to at once is
  the better design for a reader of bilingual sources, and it is correct.
- **Labels on by default.** Refused with a stated reason. I have proposed the blurb-on-first-load
  route to the same end instead (B3).
- **Hatching occupied China.** Removed at the author's request. If the flat salmon still needs to
  read as a weaker claim, a stipple or an inner dashed edge is the live option.
- **Permalinks, shared views, or a shareable quiz level.** All exist, including the level in the
  layer bitfield.
- **Drawing the Communist base areas** — done, at 75 areas. **Drawing all sub-units at once** —
  refused for good reason ("fifteen hundred lines"). **"Greatest extent" wording** — fixed.
  **Long context in tooltips** — refused as a class, correctly; everything I propose above goes in
  the detail card or a panel, never a tooltip.
- **Photographs or audio.** Never proposed, never rejected — but a media layer collides with the
  emailable single-file build, and there are twenty higher-value things above it.
- **More geometry.** The map is more accurate than the teaching around it can currently exploit.
  Every remaining item in `tasks.md`'s open queue is a sub-pixel question. That work is done; this
  work is not.
