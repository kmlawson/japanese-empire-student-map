Every dataset this map is drawn from, what it was used for, and where it came from. The short version: coastlines and national outlines are Natural Earth, except China's, which is the Republican province sheet and nothing else; China's provinces are the real ones of 1928–45; Korea comes from a map of the period rather than from modern units; the princely states of India were georeferenced by hand from a 1931 atlas; and the occupied zone and the line of control are traced from period maps and are approximations, as they say on the map itself.

Boundaries and coastlines

  • Natural Earth, 1:10m cultural vectors. All coastlines and national outlines outside China, and the base for the frame. Public domain — Natural Earth's terms state the data may be used in any manner, including modifying the content and design, and that no permission is needed.
    github.com/nvkelso/natural-earth-vector
  • ENP-China — Chinese provincial boundaries, 1928–1945. Every Chinese land region. These are the actual provinces of the Nanking decade and the war, not modern ones: Jehol is a province, and Suiyuan and Chahar are provinces. On the 1930 map these draw Manchuria as Fengtien, Kirin and Heilungkiang, and Jehol beside them; the 1942 map draws Manchukuo from its own sheet instead, below. CC BY 4.0 — "Provinces 1922–1928 © 2021 by ENP-China Project".
    Elites, Networks and Power in Modern China, Aix-Marseille University. Refreshed from the author's own copy in August 2026: the same twenty-nine provinces, drawn slightly finer — 20,505 points against 20,400.

    A second export of the same sheet, dissolved to one polygon, is the ground under China: 2,013 rings and 18,008 points of the Republic as a whole, coast and land frontier, laid beneath Tibet and Sinkiang on both dates and beneath Manchukuo, Mengchiang and the occupation on the second, so that where two of those disagree by a kilometre the crack shows ground rather than sea. It is drawn in a neutral colour and takes no pointer, so a sliver reads as a seam rather than as one country leaking into the next. This is what Natural Earth's outline used to do and was switched off for: it was far coarser than the provinces and drew China's shore twice, a rough line where the modern outline ran and a fine one a kilometre away where the provinces did. This one is off the same sheet as the provinces, so it does not. The 1912–21 and 1922–28 sheets are cached alongside the one in use, for any earlier snapshot. These came by way of the author's own Modern East Asia GIS project, where they had already been gathered for teaching.

    On accuracy: the sheet's coastline is finely drawn — within about a kilometre and a half of Natural Earth's — but its interior provincial boundaries are generalised, with vertices seventeen to twenty-eight kilometres apart against one to four along the coast. Measured against modern first-level boundaries, those interior lines sit a median of about nine kilometres from the boundary they stand for, and the largest disagreements are twenty-five to thirty-seven. The displacement scatters in every direction rather than leaning one way, which points to the scale at which the boundaries were drawn rather than to a coordinate error. On this map it is apparent only where an independently drawn feature crosses the same ground: the Yellow River, which formed the Shaanxi–Shanxi border, runs about thirty kilometres west of where this source puts that border. The river is in the right place. The sheet remains the best available depiction of the Republic's provinces as they stood, and is used here for what it is good at — which provinces existed, and roughly where.
  • Republican provinces from the AMS 1:250,000 sheets — the alternative offered in Layers. A second set of provincial boundaries for China, which the reader can switch to in place of ENP's. Traced by Wikimedia Commons user Lilauid from the US Army Map Service 1:250,000 China series (L500, L531, L581), the AMS 1:50,000 Fukien sheets (L783), the Republic of China 1301 series at 1:1,000,000 and the Kiangsi Provincial Land Survey Bureau's 1:50,000, with Academia Sinica's CCTS tiles as a backdrop.
    File:Republic of China edcp location map 1936.svg — CC BY-SA 4.0, which the boundaries derived from it inherit. Built on Uwe Dedering's China edcp location map (CC BY-SA 3.0 / GFDL), from which its projection comes.

    It is a drawing rather than a dataset — no coordinate system, boundaries as open lines and areas nowhere at all — so three things were done to it, by tools/roc_provinces.py. It was georeferenced from the frame its base map states (equidistant conic, central meridian 104° E, central parallel 36° N, standard parallels 30° and 42° N, the central meridian running 57.0° N to 17.96° N), which checks itself against the centre latitude that page gives to within a fifth of an arcminute; the drawn coastline then lands a median of 0.59 km from Natural Earth's. Its boundary, border and coastline layers were noded together and the faces of the resulting arrangement traversed, to make areas out of lines. And each face was named by asking the ENP sheet which province it falls in — ENP's positions are the thing being replaced, but its identities are right.

    What it buys: the interior boundaries are six to thirteen times finer, and the Shaanxi–Shanxi border follows the Yellow River — a median of 0.96 km from it, against ENP's 30.5 km. What it costs: it is dated 1936, so it predates Sikang's promotion to a province in 1939 and draws Manchuria as one disputed block rather than as provinces, for which ENP's are kept; and it is one contributor's careful tracing rather than a scholarly release, with no independent check on its accuracy beyond the two measurements above. ENP remains the default for those reasons.
  • geoBoundaries ADM1 and ADM2. The provinces ceded to Thailand in 1941, the northern Malay states, Burma by division, Kengtung by district, the Indian and Japanese first-level units, and the Indonesian residencies. CC BY 4.0.
    github.com/wmgeolab/geoBoundaries · Runfola, D. et al. (2020), "geoBoundaries: A global database of political administrative boundaries", PLoS ONE 15(4): e0231866.

    Kengtung State is the three modern districts of Kengtung, Monghsat and Tachileik exactly; the trans-Salween part of Mongpan is the eastern end of Langkho district, cut at 98.15° E. Hyderabad is the weakest of these: the Nizam's dominions also took in Marathi and Kannada districts now in Maharashtra and Karnataka, and the map says as much in the note.
  • The thirteen provinces of colonial Korea. Korea's province boundaries and its coastline, drawn without simplification because the provinces are the finer source. This is why Hwanghae is one province and Kaesong is in Keiki-dō, where it belonged. It replaces an assembly from the modern provinces of the two republics, which cannot give the period map however it is grouped: Hwanghae was one province until 1954, Ryanggang and Jagang did not exist, and Kaesong was in Keiki-dō rather than in Hwanghae.
    spatialhistory.net/yale/done/korea.html

    How it is read: the page is an SVG export in an equirectangular projection whose parameters it states in its own comments, so tools/fetch_korea_1930.py turns the drawing back into longitude and latitude. The residual offset was measured against Natural Earth's coastline over 253 matched points and corrected. Jeju, which the province layer omits, is taken from the land layer and put back into South Chŏlla.
  • The princely states of India, 1931. Forty-one polygons of the states as they stood in 1931, georeferenced by the author of this map from the atlas volume of the Imperial Gazetteer of India, 1931. They replaced five modern Indian states standing in for six hundred princely ones — Telangana for Hyderabad, Karnataka for Mysore — an approximation the About text had to apologise for.
    dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/gaz_atlas_1931/

    The file itself names nine of the forty-one. The rest are identified in PRINCELY_NAMES in tools/build_map.py by where they are, which for the large ones is unmistakable; anything not in that table answers with the territory rather than with a guess.

    The forty-first is the North-East Frontier Tract, 91.5–97.8° E and 27.0–29.2° N, added to the layer in August 2026 along with a slight refinement of the Madras States. It is drawn, and it is worth knowing that none of it falls inside the traced outline of British India — that tracing leaves the frontier tracts out, which is the same reason the Indian provinces are not drawn. So the tract is shaded on ground the map otherwise gives to Tibet, exactly as the 1931 atlas shades it and unlike the 1931 sheet the outline comes from. It is unnamed in the source and so answers with the territory rather than with a guess.
  • British India in 1931, and the European enclaves in it, traced. The outline of the Raj, the ten French and Portuguese settlements inside it, and Sikkim and Bhutan, all traced by the author of this map from a 1931 sheet. The Raj replaces modern India, Pakistan and Bangladesh standing in for it — a stand-in that put the whole of Kashmir, Arunachal Pradesh and the Chittagong tracts inside the 1931 frontier and drew the line of Partition nowhere. One outline of 13,195 points with eleven holes in it for the enclaves and the protectorates; the ten settlements are named from the tracing itself, which is what lets the pointer tell Dadrá from Nagar Aveli and Karikal from Yanaon, where before there was one shape called Dadra & Nagar Haveli and no shape at all for four of the five French ones.

    The provinces of British India are not drawn. The only source for them was the modern first-level units, and measured against the tracing a tenth of their vertices fall outside it — Arunachal Pradesh by up to 107 km, Mizoram by 51, Ladakh by 45, which are exactly the frontier tracts the tracing leaves out. Drawing them would have put the Raj a hundred kilometres past its own line wherever the Administrative layer was switched on. The princely states above are drawn over it as before.

    How closely it is drawn. A tracing is not a survey file: it has the vertices somebody chose to put in it, and thinning it by the band a country of India's size earns had moved the drawn line a median of 2.3 pixels from the tracing at the deepest zoom the map allows, and 7 at the ninetieth percentile. It is held to the map's own standard instead — half a pixel at 40× — which comes to 5,358 points drawn out of the 10,623 inside the frame, and 72 KB. Measured back against the tracing, the drawn line sits a median of 0.07 pixels from it, 0.26 at the ninetieth, 0.47 at the ninety-ninth and 0.61 at its worst — all of it inside half a pixel at the deepest zoom. The holes are drawn whole, every point of them: they are a dozen points each and thinning them at a band meant for a coastline shrank them until the small-shape sieve threw them away. Of the enclaves, 297 of Portuguese India's 361 points are drawn and 739 of French India's 747.
  • The North China Area Army's own security map, September 1942 — the alternative reading offered in Layers. 付図第五「北支那方面軍占拠地域内治安概況（昭和十七年九月中）」, an appendix to 防衛庁防衛研修所戦史室 編『北支の治安戦＜2＞』(戦史叢書 第50巻, 東京: 朝雲新聞社, 1971), held by the National Institute for Defense Studies and readable there in full. Traced by the author of this map and published as kmlawson.github.io/1942-occupation-map; the tracing is in the public domain. Two of the sheet's three categories are areas and are drawn here: pacified 治安地区, 84 rings and 3,099 vertices over about 275,000 km², and un-pacified 未治安地区, 53 rings and 1,819 vertices over about 182,000 km². The third, semi-pacified 準治安地区, is what the sheet leaves blank, and it is left blank here, so unshaded ground inside north China is not a claim that nobody was there.
    nids.mod.go.jp/military_history_search/SoshoView?kanno=050

    This is a different claim from the shading it replaces, by a different author, and the two are never drawn together. The traced 1940 zone is where Japanese authority reached at all; this is where the army itself thought it had the country in hand, written down by the people doing the fighting. It covers north China alone — 108–122.6° E, 33–42° N, that army's area of responsibility — so choosing it leaves the Yangtze valley, Canton, Amoy and Hainan unshaded and hides the line of control, which is the inland edge of the other reading's zone. The Communist base areas from Wu Yuexing's atlas are hidden with it: they are a third author answering a third question, and the un-pacified areas are the same ground read differently.
  • Nepal, Afghanistan, Sikkim and Bhutan, 1931. Traced by the author of this map from the same 1931 Imperial Gazetteer sheet as British India, so that a country and its neighbour are drawn from one source and meet along one line. Nepal is 96 vertices, Afghanistan 420, Sikkim 23 and Bhutan 38. They replace Natural Earth's modern outlines and, for the three Himalayan states, the Wuhan project's layers. Afghanistan is not a country this map names — it is drawn as Elsewhere, with the rest of the ground beyond the frame of the story — but its line is now the period one.
  • Modern East Asia GIS. British Weihaiwei and French Kwangchowan, from the author's own QGIS project. Sikkim, Bhutan and Nepal were drawn from it too until August 2026 and now come from the 1931 Imperial Gazetteer tracings below; Tannu Tuva likewise, and now comes from the 1940 Japanese sheet. These layers are stored in an azimuthal-equidistant grid centred on Wuhan, on the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid, and are converted back to longitude and latitude here by solving the geodesic direct problem — which is what an azimuthal-equidistant grid is: a true bearing and a true distance from the centre. They were previously inverted with the spherical formula, which put every one of them between two and six kilometres out of place, radially away from Wuhan.
  • Christmas Island is taken from Natural Earth's ne_10m_admin_0_countries, where it is filed with the Cocos (Keeling) group as "Indian Ocean Territories" — an Australian arrangement of 1958 and not one of either of this map's dates. It is drawn here as what it was: a Straits Settlement, annexed in 1900 and run from Singapore, and Japanese-occupied from 31 March 1942.
  • OpenStreetMap. The fine coastlines of the Ryukyus, the Bonins, the Volcano and Izu Islands, and of Micronesia and Melanesia — the Marianas, Palau, the Carolines with Truk, Ponape and Kusaie, the Marshalls, the Bismarcks, Bougainville, the Solomons, the Louisiades and the Gilberts — and the names of nineteen hundred islands in them, with the group each belongs to. Every island of the Okinawa, Miyako, Yaeyama and Senkaku groups large enough to draw names itself; so do Iwo Jima, Saipan, Truk's islands, Peleliu, Kwajalein, Tarawa, Guadalcanal and Bougainville. Where the name in use today is not the name of the period, both are given — Kwajalein (Kuwajleen), Ponape (Pohnpei), Dublon (Tonowas). About a third are reefs OSM does not name, and those give their group instead. These are fetched only when the map is zoomed deep into one of those places, so a reader who never leaves the mainland never downloads them. The island names come from an Overpass query for place=island and place=islet, cached in tools/cache/ so the build does not depend on the network.
    The islands of the South China Sea come from the same file and are fetched the same way: the Spratlys, the Paracels and Pratas, 78 features in all, with the largest island of each group also carried on the base map so that the group can be pointed at before the rest arrives. These are traced from present-day shapes, which does not reflect more recent land reclamation.
    Two more windows were added the same way: Singapore and its islands, and Ulleung with the Liancourt Rocks — the second with its Korean and Japanese names and a note on the dispute today, the first with the caution that its coastline is the modern one and Jurong Island was made by joining seven smaller ones after 1995.
    The same coastlines also give the four outer island groups the map had no shape for at all: the Turtle and Mangsee Islands in the Sulu Sea, Miangas (the Island of Palmas of the 1928 arbitration) and the Cocos (Keeling) Islands. Those are traced out of the split-coastlines shapefile — 876,182 linestrings and 1.2 GB — by tools/extract_coast.py, which reads each record's own bounding box out of the .shp header and seeks past the geometry when it misses, which is 876,000 times out of 876,182, and chains the pieces that share an end back into closed islands; the 37 KB that can be drawn is cached and committed, the source is not.
    And the central Pacific, which the bulk extract does not reach: it stops at 176.85 E, the eastern edge of the Gilberts, so the Ellice Islands, the Phoenix group, Tokelau, Swains, the two northern Cook atolls and the American specks on the equator had one Natural Earth ring apiece and no more. Seven windows out of the same shapefile give 411 closed rings — 130 of them above the five-hectare floor — with the islet names from Overpass: Fongafale and Funafala on Funafuti, Nikumaroro and Rawaki and Enderbury in the Phoenix group, Atafu's motu, Pukapuka and Nassau. Rotuma came with them and is left out, being Fijian and Fiji not being on this map. Of the 16,233 vertices that clear the floor, 583 are drawn — 3.6%, against 14.9% for the South China Sea extract through the same pipeline, because the tolerance is 0.002° and these atolls are surveyed at about ten metres, so what is dropped is a fifth of a pixel at the deepest zoom the map allows.

Rivers

  • Natural Earth, 1:10m rivers and lake centrelines. The Yangtze and the Yellow River. Public domain. Two hand-drawn tails were needed: Natural Earth's Yangtze centreline stops about 200 km short of the sea, and its Yellow River stops short of the Gulf of Chihli.
  • The 1938–47 course of the Yellow River is redrawn from the channel map at disasterhistory.org (Chris Courtney, after Saito et al. 2000): out of the breach below Chengchow, down the Chia-lu into the Ying, down the Ying into the Huai, and through Hungtse Lake and the Grand Canal into the Yangtze above Chinkiang. For nine years the Yellow River reached the sea through the Yangtze's mouth.
    disasterhistory.org

The occupation and the line of control

  • The Pacific mandates — Japan's South Seas Mandate, Australia's Territory of New Guinea and the British mandate over Nauru — are traced from a 1927 chart of the three Class C League of Nations mandates over the former German Pacific. They are drawn as outlines and not as filled areas, because a mandate over an ocean is a line on a chart: almost everything inside them is water, and the islands they are about are specks. They appear on the 1930 map only. The same chart supplies the boundary between the Australian mandate and Papua across New Guinea, which used to be a straight line drawn by hand and is now the traced one.
  • Manchukuo is traced from 滿洲國地圖 1935, published by the research section of the South Manchuria Railway Company (南滿洲鐡道株式會社資料課) — the company that was the instrument of the state it is mapping. Two layers came out of it: the state as a single outline, which is what the map draws with the Administrative layer off, and its fourteen provinces for when that layer is on. It replaces the Republican provinces the 1942 map used to assemble Manchukuo from, and is the only period source here for Manchukuo's own provincial scheme — the four Hsingan provinces of the eastern Mongol leagues, Chientao on the Korean border, and Jehol, taken from the Republic in 1933. Those provinces are shown on the 1942 map alone: on the 1930 map Manchuria is Chinese, Jehol is a province of the Republic standing outside it, and this scheme did not yet exist.
  • The Kwantung Leased Territory is traced by hand from a 1935 sheet: the mainland in one ring of 227 points and nineteen islands with it, every vertex of the 425 kept. It replaces a cut made with a straight line across the isthmus and a bounding box, which was the 1898 lease boundary in the right place but gave the leasehold a coast that was Liaoning's with corners taken off it — half-plane clipping is convex-only — and Manchuria's own filler showed through each one as a fleck of the wrong colour.
  • The occupied coast is stroked in its own colour. Every shape on this map is painted fill and stroke in the same colour, a 1.3-pixel line that does not scale with the zoom, which is what closes the hairline between two neighbours drawn from different files. At a coast it fattens the shore into the sea instead. The occupied zone is the one shape drawn through a clip — cut to China's land — so its own stroke stops at the waterline while China's does not, and Free China's yellow ran as a thread one pixel wide along the whole occupied shore. China's filler is therefore not stroked at all: its outline is stroked by two paths instead, yellow where the occupation does not reach the coast and salmon where it does, so the number of stroked points on the map is unchanged. Whether the occupation reaches a stretch of coast is asked of the land three kilometres inside it, not of the distance to the traced edge — where the answer is no, the strip between that edge and the sea is Free China's and its coast should be yellow.
  • Water traced round both leaseholds. Kwantung and Kwangchowwan are drawn from their own tracings and the country under them is Natural Earth's coarse outline, which at this scale is simply a different coastline. Round Kwangchowwan it painted the arms of Guangzhou Bay as land, so yellow China showed in the channels between the leasehold's six pieces; round the Liaodong tip it ran a little outside the traced leasehold along the whole shore and showed as a rim. Two hand-traced rings say where the water is: everything inside them that the leasehold itself does not cover is painted as water, with the leasehold's own rings held back as even-odd holes so nothing paints over them. Measured by rendered colour inside the rings: 655 yellow pixels at Kwantung and 1,177 at Kwangchowwan before, none after.
  • Mengchiang and Outer Mongolia are traced from 支那全土並附近大地圖・欧洲現勢大地圖 (Shina zendo narabi fukin daichizu · Ōshū gensei daichizu), a Japanese sheet of 1940, rather than assembled out of Chahar and Suiyuan, which is what stood in for Mengchiang before. Mengchiang is three pieces and 887 vertices on this sheet against one piece and 358 on the tracing it replaces, and it reaches further west — to 103.9° E against 108.7°. The three pieces are the governments the state was federated out of, and the Administrative layer names them: the Mongol leagues, the North Shansi (Jinbei) Administration around Tatung, and the South Chahar (Chanan) Administration around Hsuanhua. Each is claimed by a town that can only be in one of them. Kalgan is not used for that, though it was Chanan's seat: this sheet draws it inside the Mongol block, which is also where the federation as a whole was governed from. Outer Mongolia is one ring of 550 vertices and replaces Natural Earth's modern outline of the same country, and Tannu Tuva one ring of 96, replacing the Wuhan project's layer of 762 — a smaller country as this sheet draws it, 162,000 km² against 171,000, and set about a degree further east. Both are dated 1940, two years before this map's second date; the note on Mengchiang says what that means for a frontier that moved. What is left of those two provinces outside the traced line is Free China's, and that is how the map now draws them.
  • The occupied zone in China is traced from a period map of the occupation rather than drawn as whole provinces, and adjusted to December 1942. The maps used are held in occupation-maps/, with a README giving each file's Commons page, licence and author; the principal one is the 1940 sheet of the China 1900–1949 series, which is in the public domain. The dates the cities fell come from the Sino-Japanese War card set in the module's own timeline review tool. It is an approximation and a generous one: Japanese control ran along the railways and around the cities, and much of the countryside inside the line was held by Communist and Nationalist guerrillas.
  • The dashed line on the Dec 1942 map is the extent of Japanese control and the fighting front at that date. It is not taken from any single map: several were used in settling where it should run, among them the map captioned "War in the Pacific" in Andrew Gordon, A Modern History of Japan: From Tokugawa Times to the Present (Oxford University Press), whose own legend calls it a greatest extent. It is corrected in New Guinea against Dudley McCarthy, South-West Pacific Area — First Year: Kokoda to Wau (Australia in the War of 1939–1945, AWM 1959) and Samuel Milner, Victory in Papua: Japan held the north coast and the far west, and in Papua only the Buna–Sanananda beachhead. Merauke was Dutch for the whole war. It is labelled here for its date instead, because December 1942 is not a maximum — the naval perimeter was widest in July and August, the area of China under Japanese control was largest in 1944, and Kwangchowwan, the northern Malay states and direct rule in Indochina all came later.

    Only the parts that were not a land frontier are drawn by hand — the front in China, the ocean perimeter, and the cut across the Papuan peninsula north of Port Moresby. Everywhere the limit followed a real border, the line is lifted straight off the territory outlines, so it sits exactly on the Manchukuo, Mengchiang, Burma and Indochina frontiers rather than floating near them. In China the line is the inland edge of the shaded occupied zone, taken off that zone's own block and smoothed the same way, so that the two cannot drift apart: a perimeter claiming ground the shading did not was the worse of the two errors. See EXTENT_* in tools/build_map.py.
  • The Communist base areas and guerrilla zones, 1941–42, are traced from sheet 199 of 武月星主編，《中國抗日戰爭史地圖集：1931–1945》 (Wu Yuexing, ed., Historical Atlas of China's War of Resistance against Japan, 1931–1945). Seventy-five areas, from the Shaan-Gan-Ning border region round Yenan to the coastal pockets of Shantung and the New Fourth Army country along the lower Yangtze. They are drawn over the occupied shading and not instead of it, which is the point of them: almost all of this ground lies inside the line the Japanese army had drawn round itself. Their extent moved from month to month and the "mopping-up" campaigns of 1941–42 cut some of them badly, so this is one atlas's reading of two years, not a line anyone held.
  • The line of control in south China encloses the coastal enclaves by passing inland of them, which is the way round the perimeter has to go: all along this coast Free China is on the landward side and the sea, which the navy had, on the other. It used to take the seaward arc, and every one of the enclaves it was drawn for — Canton, Fatshan, Kongmoon, Amoy, Kinmen, Swatow, Chaochow, Chaoyang — was outside the line on a map that shaded them as occupied. Where one traced ring is the whole block, as at Canton, the detour follows that ring's own boundary offset nine kilometres; where the block is two or three separate blobs, as at Amoy and at Swatow, it is a hull round them. Macao is cut out of it: Portugal was neutral and Macao stayed Portuguese for the whole war, so its own ring is wound against the perimeter and reads as a hole, and the dashed line draws a small loop round the enclave instead of swallowing it.
  • Saharat Thai Doem — Kengtung and the trans-Salween Shan states — follows the map by Xufanc on Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0, taken district by district rather than cut off with a straight line. File:Saharat_Thai_Doem_map.png shows the territory Japan actually granted rather than everything Thailand claimed in the Shan and Karenni states.

French Indochina is cut into Tonkin, Annam and Cochinchina along straight lines at roughly the historical boundaries, with Cambodia and Laos taken whole. Both those cuts, and the Papua–New Guinea line before the 1927 chart replaced it, are approximations of boundaries that in reality followed watersheds.

Kinmen (Quemoy) is not drawn as part of the colony of Taiwan. Natural Earth puts it in Taiwan because it is governed from Taipei today; it belonged to Fujian throughout the colonial period, and it is drawn with China here. The Pescadores, which were ceded with Taiwan in 1895, stay with the colony.

Territories that were one polity are drawn as one shape with no internal boundary, because there was none at the time: British India has no line at the Punjab — and no line anywhere else either, its provinces being left undrawn for want of a source that fits the traced outline — French Indochina none at the Mekong, Korea none at the 38th parallel, and in 1930 Burma is inside British India, where it was until 1937.

Everything outside China uses modern coastlines and national outlines as a stand-in for the historical ones. That is fine for coastlines and rough for land borders.

What was done to the data

Simplification goes by the size of each ring rather than by one tolerance for a whole country, so a fifty-kilometre island is not flattened by a setting chosen for Luzon. Korea, Kengtung and the princely states are drawn at the full detail of their sources, and the hand-traced outlines — British India, the enclaves in it, Kwantung, Manchukuo, Mengchiang — are held to half a pixel at the deepest zoom rather than to the band their size would otherwise earn. Where a territory is assembled from sub-units, a whole-country shape is laid beneath them all so that a crack between two neighbours drawn from different files shows the country rather than the sea.

Some boundaries are hand-traced constants rather than data: the occupied zone, the ocean stretches of the line of control, the Kwantung lease across the Liaodong isthmus, the cut at Paotow, Chandernagore, Wake Island, and the estuary tails on both rivers. These are marked as such in tools/build_map.py, where each carries a note saying what it is and why it is drawn by hand.

The pipeline, in order, all of it in tools/build_map.py:

  1. Projection. Web Mercator, on a Pacific-centred frame running 66°E–206°E and 13°S–55°N — British India to Pearl Harbor, Sakhalin to northern Australia. Longitudes are normalised to 0–360 so the map is continuous across the antimeridian.
  2. Grouping. Units are grouped into atoms, the smallest regions any snapshot needs. Chinese provinces map straight onto atoms by name; everything else is grouped by country, with a few groups split by ring (Sakhalin at the 50th parallel, the Ryukyus and Bonins out of Japan, the Andamans out of India, Hawaii and the Aleutians out of the United States). The modern outline of China is also kept, drawn beneath the provinces: the two sources put the land border in slightly different places, and without something underneath those disagreements open as slivers of sea at deep zoom.
  3. Dissolving. Shared boundaries inside an atom are removed by cancelling directed edges that appear in both directions, and the survivors stitched back into rings. Where the source data is not topologically clean the stitch fails and the original rings are kept; the same-colour stroke used in rendering hides the seam either way.
  4. Clipping and simplification. Rings are clipped to the frame with Sutherland–Hodgman and thinned with Douglas–Peucker at a tolerance of about half a pixel, less on small islands.
  5. Minimum sizes. Islands smaller than a fingertip keep a minimum-size disc so they stay visible and tappable.

Output: japan-empire-map.svg and its companions, geometry only — no names, no colours, no history. All of that comes from texts/.

Names

English gives the name as it was used at the time, with the present-day name after it where they differ: Taihoku (Taipei), Batavia (Jakarta). Everywhere in China it is the other way round, and in Pinyin with its tone marks — Shěnyáng (Mukden), Chóngqìng (Chungking), Běijīng (Peking / Peiping), Shāndōng (Shantung), Guǎngzhōuwān (Kwangchowan) — because a student meets the period spelling in what they read about the war and the present-day one on every map they can look it up in, and the second is the one that will find it. Places whose English name is an English phrase rather than a romanisation keep it: the Kwantung Leased Territory, the Republic of China, Manchukuo. Japanese gives the imperial-period form — 京城 Keijō for Seoul, 奉天 Hōten for Mukden, 新京 Shinkyō for Changchun, 昭南島 Shōnantō for Singapore, 樺太 Karafuto for southern Sakhalin, 大宮島 Ōmiyajima for Guam. Chinese is in traditional characters, as the period used them; Korean is in hangul with McCune–Reischauer romanisation, and Sino-Korean readings are used for places in the Chinese and Japanese spheres, which is how they were and are read in Korean — 奉天 as 봉천 Pongch'ŏn, 上海 as 상해 Sanghae.

Each place carries only the scripts that belong to it. Hangul on Korea; a Japanese reading on Japan proper, the colonies, the Manchurian group and the South Seas Mandate; neither on places that answered to neither. British India is in English alone, Thailand in English and Thai, Portuguese Timor in English and Portuguese, and the Netherlands Indies in English and Dutch with Japanese on the cities and islands Japan occupied and named.

A fifth field, orig, holds the local endonym where it is neither Chinese nor Korean — Thai for Bangkok, Vietnamese for Hanoi, Malay for Singapore. It is kept for reference and is not shown in the interface.

Names that changed between the two dates are held in texts/sites/overrides-1930.csv and texts/territories/sub-units/overrides-*.csv, so the 1930 map does not call Singapore Syonan-to or describe Changchun as the capital of Manchukuo.

Historical content

The place list

Drawn from the three module timelines used in the course — Modern Japan 1850–1989; late Edo to Meiji, 1850–1912; and Taishō to the end of the Occupation, 1912–1952. Those handouts are not included in this repository. Every city, battle and incident on the map corresponds to an event in those timelines, plus the territories needed to make sense of them.

The browse layer

texts/browse.csv holds about a hundred and seventy cities that are context rather than content: the provincial capitals of Republican China and Manchukuo, and the larger cities of Korea, Taiwan, Japan and Southeast Asia. They are drawn smaller and grey, beneath the markers the quiz uses, and are never examined. English names are the period ones with the modern name in brackets where they differ — Paoting (Baoding), Taikyu (Taegu), Kirun (Keelung), Jesselton (Kota Kinabalu).

Also examined, not used

From the same Modern East Asia GIS project: Korea Pre-1945.gpkg and modern-east-asia-gis.gpkg (layers for Korea, Karafuto, the Andamans and Kiribati). These turned out to be Natural Earth extracts cut the same way this build already cuts them — merged Korea, Sakhalin south of the parallel, the Andaman rings out of India — so they were checked and set aside rather than imported. The georeferenced historical map scans in the same folder are raster and not usable as vector boundaries.
