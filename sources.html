Sources

Every dataset this map is drawn from, what it was used for, and where it came from. The short version: coastlines and national outlines are Natural Earth, except China's, which is the Republican province sheet and nothing else; China's provinces are the real ones of 1928–45; Korea comes from a map of the period rather than from modern units; the princely states of India were georeferenced by hand from a 1931 atlas; and the occupied zone and the line of control are traced from period maps and are approximations, as they say on the map itself.

Boundaries and coastlines

  • Natural Earth, 1:10m cultural vectors. All coastlines and national outlines outside China, and the base for the frame. Public domain — Natural Earth's terms state the data may be used in any manner, including modifying the content and design, and that no permission is needed.
    github.com/nvkelso/natural-earth-vector
  • ENP-China — Chinese provincial boundaries, 1928–1945. Every Chinese land region. These are the actual provinces of the Nanking decade and the war, not modern ones: Jehol is a province, Suiyuan and Chahar are provinces, and Manchukuo is Fengtien, Kirin and Heilungkiang. CC BY 4.0 — "Provinces 1922–1928 © 2021 by ENP-China Project".
    Elites, Networks and Power in Modern China, Aix-Marseille University.

    On accuracy: the sheet's coastline is finely drawn — within about a kilometre and a half of Natural Earth's — but its interior provincial boundaries are generalised, with vertices seventeen to twenty-eight kilometres apart against one to four along the coast. Measured against modern first-level boundaries, those interior lines sit a median of about nine kilometres from the boundary they stand for, and the largest disagreements are twenty-five to thirty-seven. The displacement scatters in every direction rather than leaning one way, which points to the scale at which the boundaries were drawn rather than to a coordinate error. On this map it is apparent only where an independently drawn feature crosses the same ground: the Yellow River, which formed the Shaanxi–Shanxi border, runs about thirty kilometres west of where this source puts that border. The river is in the right place. The sheet remains the best available depiction of the Republic's provinces as they stood, and is used here for what it is good at — which provinces existed, and roughly where.
  • Republican provinces from the AMS 1:250,000 sheets — the alternative offered in Layers. A second set of provincial boundaries for China, which the reader can switch to in place of ENP's. Traced by Wikimedia Commons user Lilauid from the US Army Map Service 1:250,000 China series (L500, L531, L581), the AMS 1:50,000 Fukien sheets (L783), the Republic of China 1301 series at 1:1,000,000 and the Kiangsi Provincial Land Survey Bureau's 1:50,000, with Academia Sinica's CCTS tiles as a backdrop.
    File:Republic of China edcp location map 1936.svg — CC BY-SA 4.0, which the boundaries derived from it inherit. Built on Uwe Dedering's China edcp location map (CC BY-SA 3.0 / GFDL), from which its projection comes.

    It is a drawing rather than a dataset — no coordinate system, boundaries as open lines and areas nowhere at all — so three things were done to it, by tools/roc_provinces.py. It was georeferenced from the frame its base map states (equidistant conic, central meridian 104° E, central parallel 36° N, standard parallels 30° and 42° N, the central meridian running 57.0° N to 17.96° N), which checks itself against the centre latitude that page gives to within a fifth of an arcminute; the drawn coastline then lands a median of 0.59 km from Natural Earth's. Its boundary, border and coastline layers were noded together and the faces of the resulting arrangement traversed, to make areas out of lines. And each face was named by asking the ENP sheet which province it falls in — ENP's positions are the thing being replaced, but its identities are right.

    What it buys: the interior boundaries are six to thirteen times finer, and the Shaanxi–Shanxi border follows the Yellow River — a median of 0.96 km from it, against ENP's 30.5 km. What it costs: it is dated 1936, so it predates Sikang's promotion to a province in 1939 and draws Manchuria as one disputed block rather than as provinces, for which ENP's are kept; and it is one contributor's careful tracing rather than a scholarly release, with no independent check on its accuracy beyond the two measurements above. ENP remains the default for those reasons.
  • geoBoundaries ADM1 and ADM2. The provinces ceded to Thailand in 1941, the northern Malay states, Burma by division, Kengtung by district, the Indian and Japanese first-level units, and the Indonesian residencies. CC BY 4.0.
    github.com/wmgeolab/geoBoundaries · Runfola, D. et al. (2020), "geoBoundaries: A global database of political administrative boundaries", PLoS ONE 15(4): e0231866.
  • The thirteen provinces of colonial Korea. Korea's province boundaries and its coastline, drawn without simplification because the provinces are the finer source. This is why Hwanghae is one province and Kaesong is in Keiki-dō, where it belonged.
    spatialhistory.net/yale/done/korea.html
  • The princely states of India, 1931. Forty polygons of the states as they stood in 1931, georeferenced by the author of this map from the atlas volume of the Imperial Gazetteer of India, 1931. They replaced five modern Indian states standing in for six hundred princely ones.
    dsal.uchicago.edu/reference/gaz_atlas_1931/
  • Modern East Asia GIS. Tannu Tuva, British Weihaiwei, French Kwangchowan, Sikkim, Nepal and Bhutan, from the author's own QGIS project. These layers are stored in an azimuthal-equidistant grid centred on Wuhan, on the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid, and are converted back to longitude and latitude here by solving the geodesic direct problem — which is what an azimuthal-equidistant grid is: a true bearing and a true distance from the centre. They were previously inverted with the spherical formula, which put every one of them between two and six kilometres out of place, radially away from Wuhan.
  • Christmas Island is taken from Natural Earth's ne_10m_admin_0_countries, where it is filed with the Cocos (Keeling) group as "Indian Ocean Territories" — an Australian arrangement of 1958 and not one of either of this map's dates. It is drawn here as what it was: a Straits Settlement, annexed in 1900 and run from Singapore, and Japanese-occupied from 31 March 1942.
  • OpenStreetMap. The fine coastlines of the Ryukyus, the Bonins, the Volcano and Izu Islands, and of Micronesia and Melanesia — the Marianas, Palau, the Carolines with Truk, Ponape and Kusaie, the Marshalls, the Bismarcks, Bougainville, the Solomons, the Louisiades and the Gilberts — and the names of nineteen hundred islands in them, with the group each belongs to. Every island of the Okinawa, Miyako, Yaeyama and Senkaku groups large enough to draw names itself; so do Iwo Jima, Saipan, Truk's islands, Peleliu, Kwajalein, Tarawa, Guadalcanal and Bougainville. Where the name in use today is not the name of the period, both are given — Kwajalein (Kuwajleen), Ponape (Pohnpei), Dublon (Tonowas). About a third are reefs OSM does not name, and those give their group instead. These are fetched only when the map is zoomed deep into one of those places, so a reader who never leaves the mainland never downloads them. The island names come from an Overpass query for place=island and place=islet, cached in tools/cache/ so the build does not depend on the network.

    openstreetmap.org/copyright

Rivers

  • Natural Earth, 1:10m rivers and lake centrelines. The Yangtze and the Yellow River. Public domain. Two hand-drawn tails were needed: Natural Earth's Yangtze centreline stops about 200 km short of the sea, and its Yellow River stops short of the Gulf of Chihli.
  • The 1938–47 course of the Yellow River is redrawn from the channel map at disasterhistory.org (Chris Courtney, after Saito et al. 2000): out of the breach below Chengchow, down the Chia-lu into the Ying, down the Ying into the Huai, and through Hungtse Lake and the Grand Canal into the Yangtze above Chinkiang. For nine years the Yellow River reached the sea through the Yangtze's mouth.
    disasterhistory.org

The occupation and the line of control

  • The occupied zone in China is traced from a period map of the occupation rather than drawn as whole provinces, and adjusted to December 1942. It is an approximation and a generous one: Japanese control ran along the railways and around the cities, and much of the countryside inside the line was held by Communist and Nationalist guerrillas.
  • The dashed line on the Dec 1942 map is the extent of Japanese control and the fighting front at that date. It is not taken from any single map: several were used in settling where it should run, among them the map captioned "War in the Pacific" in Andrew Gordon, A Modern History of Japan: From Tokugawa Times to the Present (Oxford University Press), whose own legend calls it a greatest extent. It is corrected in New Guinea against Dudley McCarthy, South-West Pacific Area — First Year: Kokoda to Wau (Australia in the War of 1939–1945, AWM 1959) and Samuel Milner, Victory in Papua: Japan held the north coast and the far west, and in Papua only the Buna–Sanananda beachhead. Merauke was Dutch for the whole war. It is labelled here for its date instead, because December 1942 is not a maximum — the naval perimeter was widest in July and August, the area of China under Japanese control was largest in 1944, and Kwangchowwan, the northern Malay states and direct rule in Indochina all came later.
  • The Communist base areas and guerrilla zones, 1941–42, are traced from sheet 199 of 武月星主編，《中國抗日戰爭史地圖集：1931–1945》 (Wu Yuexing, ed., Historical Atlas of China's War of Resistance against Japan, 1931–1945). Seventy-five areas, from the Shaan-Gan-Ning border region round Yenan to the coastal pockets of Shantung and the New Fourth Army country along the lower Yangtze. They are drawn over the occupied shading and not instead of it, which is the point of them: almost all of this ground lies inside the line the Japanese army had drawn round itself. Their extent moved from month to month and the "mopping-up" campaigns of 1941–42 cut some of them badly, so this is one atlas's reading of two years, not a line anyone held.
  • Saharat Thai Doem — Kengtung and the trans-Salween Shan states — follows the map by Xufanc on Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0, taken district by district rather than cut off with a straight line.

What was done to the data

Simplification goes by the size of each ring rather than by one tolerance for a whole country, so a fifty-kilometre island is not flattened by a setting chosen for Luzon. Korea, Kengtung and the princely states are drawn at the full detail of their sources. Where a territory is assembled from sub-units, a whole-country shape is laid beneath them all so that a crack between two neighbours drawn from different files shows the country rather than the sea.

Some boundaries are hand-traced constants rather than data: the occupied zone, the ocean stretches of the line of control, the Kwantung lease across the Liaodong isthmus, the cut at Paotow, Chandernagore, Wake Island, and the estuary tails on both rivers. These are marked as such in tools/build_map.py, where each carries a note saying what it is and why it is drawn by hand.

Names

English gives the name as it was used at the time, with the present-day name after it where they differ: Taihoku (Taipei), Batavia (Jakarta). Everywhere in China it is the other way round, and in Pinyin with its tone marks — Shěnyáng (Mukden), Chóngqìng (Chungking), Běijīng (Peking / Peiping), Shāndōng (Shantung), Guǎngzhōuwān (Kwangchowan) — because a student meets the period spelling in what they read about the war and the present-day one on every map they can look it up in, and the second is the one that will find it. Places whose English name is an English phrase rather than a romanisation keep it: the Kwantung Leased Territory, the Republic of China, Manchukuo. Japanese gives the imperial-period form. Chinese is in traditional characters, as the period used them; Korean is in hangul with McCune–Reischauer romanisation.

Each place carries only the scripts that belong to it. Hangul on Korea; a Japanese reading on Japan proper, the colonies, the Manchurian group and the South Seas Mandate; neither on places that answered to neither. British India is in English alone, Thailand in English and Thai, Portuguese Timor in English and Portuguese, and the Netherlands Indies in English and Dutch with Japanese on the cities and islands Japan occupied and named.
