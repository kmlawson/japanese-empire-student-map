About this map

This interactive map is for practising the geography of the Japanese Empire: the home islands, the formal colonies, the occupied territories and puppet states, the neighbouring countries, and the cities, ports and battlefields that recur in the history of modern Japan from Perry's arrival in 1853 to the surrender in 1945.

How to use it

  • 1930 / Dec 1942 — which map you are looking at. In 1930 the colours show whose empire each place belonged to, on the eve of the Manchurian Incident. In December 1942 they show how Japan held what it had taken.
  • Explore — hover over, or tap, any territory or marker to see what it is, what it was called, when it changed hands, and why it matters.
  • Names in four scripts — every script a place answers to, shown at once rather than chosen between. The headline is English, giving the name as it was used at the time with the present-day one after it where they differ: Taihoku (Taipei), Keijō (Seoul), Batavia (Jakarta). Everywhere in China it is the other way round, and in pinyin with its tone marks — Shěnyáng (Mukden), Běijīng (Peking / Peiping), Chóngqìng (Chungking) — because a student meets the period spelling in what they read about the war and the present-day one on every map they can look it up in, and the second is the one that will find it. Under the headline come the others, and only those that belong to the place: Japanese in the imperial-period form — Keijō for Seoul, Shinkyō for Changchun, Shōnantō for Singapore — Chinese in traditional characters, as the period used them, Korean in hangul with McCune–Reischauer romanisation, and, where the place answers to a name in none of those, the local one: Thai for Bangkok, Vietnamese for Hanoi, Malay for Singapore, Aleut for Attu. Explanatory notes stay in English throughout, as do the names of the provinces of Siam and the Philippines, which is how the period's own maps of them are lettered.

A caution about borders

Inside China the boundaries are the real provinces of 1928–45, so the ground Manchukuo was made out of is Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang and Rehe as they actually stood. Mengchiang is not drawn from whole provinces at all, but traced from a map of the regime itself. Karafuto is Sakhalin south of the 50th parallel, which is where the border ran, and the Kwantung Leased Territory is traced from a 1935 sheet, mainland and islands both, rather than cut off the Liaodong peninsula with a straight line. Elsewhere, modern coastlines and outlines stand in for historical ones — fine for coasts, rough for land borders.

The area of occupied China is traced from a period map of the occupation rather than drawn as whole provinces, and adjusted to December 1942: the front runs down the Tatung–Puchow railway in Shanxi to the Yellow River bend, along the river's north bank — so Luoyang and Zhengzhou are outside it, both holding until 1944 — then down the flood course the river took after Huayuankou, round the Chinese pocket in north-west Anhui and the Dabie Shan, out to Yichang, past Yueyang, and through Zhejiang taking in Jinhua but not Quzhou or Wenzhou. It is still approximate, and it is still generous: Japanese control ran along the railways and around the cities, and much of the countryside inside the line was held by Communist and Nationalist guerrillas.

Hovering names the sub-unit under the pointer as well as the country: the province, prefecture, residency, state, division or island. These are the units of the period and they carry their period names — Kankyōhoku-dō rather than North Hamgyŏng, Tayabas rather than Quezon, Chiengmai rather than Chiang Mai, Celebes rather than Sulawesi. Their outlines, though, are assembled from modern ones — Korea excepted — so where a boundary has moved since, the shape is an approximation and the name is the thing to trust. Three places to keep in mind. Manchukuo redrew the three north-eastern provinces into its own, and the fourteen drawn here are the set on the South Manchuria Railway's sheet of 1935 — the four Hsingan provinces of the Mongol leagues, Heiho, Longjiang, Sankiang, Pinkiang, Jiandao, Fengtian, Antung, Jilin, Jinzhou, and Rehe, taken from the Republic in 1933. The 1930 map keeps the Republic's own three, Liaoning, Jilin and Heilongjiang. British India's provinces were interleaved with several hundred princely states, which are drawn from a layer of their 1931 boundaries rather than approximated from modern ones; only the smallest are below the resolution of this map, and those are drawn inside whichever province surrounded them. And Siam's changwat sat inside the monthon, the "circles" abolished in 1933, which are not drawn. Korea is the exception to all of this: its thirteen provinces come from a map of the period rather than from modern units, so Hwanghae is one province and Kaesong is in Keiki-dō, where it belonged.

Where several modern states made up one historical territory they are drawn as a single shape with no boundary between them, because there was none: British India has no line at the Punjab, French Indochina none at the Mekong, Korea none at the 38th parallel. Present-day borders that had not been drawn yet do not appear.

The front line moved constantly between 1931 and 1945, and any single date is a snapshot. For close work, use a proper historical atlas.

Sources and credits

Every dataset this map is drawn from is listed here, with what each was used for, where it came from and under what licence, and a note on what was done to the data.
