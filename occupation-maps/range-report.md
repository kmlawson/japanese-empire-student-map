# Extent of control, year by year

A companion to `report.md`. For each year it lists the large settlements under
Japanese, Mongol client or Mengjiang authority, and the shape that authority
took. The polygons in `suiyuan-history.html` are drawn from this file.

**Reading the status column.**

* **Confirmed** — a dated capture or a dated transfer of administration, sourced
  in `sources.md`.
* **Likely** — no dated capture found, but the town was a county seat or banner
  office inside a unit whose administration is attested (a Bayantala county, a
  South Chahar county, a league office), on or beside a railway or road the
  occupying force held. These are the towns a garrison army in this position
  would necessarily hold, and no source places them outside the regime.

**Reading the map.** One zone, one shade. Everything inside was under Japanese
or Mengjiang authority; what that authority consisted of varied enormously
across it, and the tables below are where that variation is recorded rather
than in the shading. Along the railway it meant garrisons, tax collection and a
civil administration; out on the Chahar, Shilingol and Ulanchab steppe it meant
a banner office with a Japanese adviser and a post on the route.

The one addition is the Hetao salient of February–March 1940, drawn separately
because it was held for six weeks and at no other time.

Two things are deliberately not drawn.

* **The Daqingshan.** The base area is carried by the events themselves — the
  march in, the sweeps, the sub-district, the worst year — which locate it
  better than a shaded band could, and a band drawn across the middle of the
  zone invites the reading that everything outside it was quiet, which is
  false.
* **Territory the regime listed but never held** — the Hetao and the Ordos.
  Discussed in `report.md` §4, where it matters. Drawing it only muddles the
  question this file is trying to answer, which is what was actually held.

Instead, the map carries a **toggleable overlay of the Suiyuan and Chahar
provincial boundaries**, so the zone can be read against the real administrative
geography: where the zone stops short of the province, where it crosses one,
and where the two coincide.

Unless a date is given, each list is the position at 31 December of that year.
Modern names follow in brackets where the name changed.

## 1933 — the seed

No Japanese occupation of either province. One Kwantung Army client
administration on Chahar soil.

| Settlement | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Dolonnor 多倫 | Confirmed | Seat of the Chahar East Special Autonomous District from 22 Sep 1933 under Li Shouxin; Kwantung Army-run, Manchukuo-funded from July 1935 |

Zone: a small district in eastern Chahar around Dolonnor. Everything else in
both provinces is under the Republic of China.

## 1935 — conceded, not occupied

Still no Japanese army of occupation in Chahar. What changed is that Chinese
regular troops left, by agreement, and the vacuum was filled by
Japanese-sponsored security forces and Mongol clients. The towns below were not
garrisoned by Japan; they were removed from Chinese military control.

| Settlement | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Dolonnor 多倫 | Confirmed | As 1933 |
| Zhangbei 張北 | Confirmed | 29th Army withdraws from the county and everything north of it under the Ching–Doihara agreement, 27 Jun 1935; 4,000 security troops take over |
| Kangbao 康保 | Likely | North of Zhangbei, inside the evacuated zone |
| Baochang 寶昌 [Taipusi Banner] | Likely | Same |
| Shangdu 商都 | Likely | Same |
| Huade 化德 | Likely | Same |
| Guyuan 沽源 | Likely | Same; the Datan accord of 2 Feb 1935 had already made ~700 km² east of the Guyuan–Dushikou line a demilitarised zone |

Zone: northern Chahar, from the escarpment above Zhangjiakou to the Chahar
banner steppe. Chinese accounts put the loss at 70–80% of the province's
territory. Suiyuan is untouched; Zhangjiakou, Datong, Guisui and Baotou are all
Chinese.

## 1938 — the occupation regime, and a hole in it

Territorially identical to the end of 1937. Two changes of substance:

* The **Mongolia Garrison Army** is formed (detachment 4 Jan, army 4 Jul) and
  the corridor is garrisoned properly rather than merely occupied.
* The **Daqingshan** becomes a contested zone from 29 July, when about 2,300
  Eighth Route Army troops march in. By December there are three guerrilla
  zones — Suizhong, Suixi, Suinan — astride the railway the whole position
  depends on. The county towns of Wuchuan, Guyang and Taolin stay in Japanese
  hands; the country between them does not.

No settlement gained, none lost.

---

## 1939 — unification, and the first probe west

Territorially identical again. On 1 September the three governments merge into
the Mengjiang United Autonomous Government, capital **Zhangjiakou**, with five
leagues, two administrative offices and two special cities.

Outside the zone, and staying outside it: the Hetao — Wuyuan 五原, Linhe 臨河,
Anbei 安北, Micang 米倉, Langshan 狼山, Yanjiang 晏江 — under the Nationalist
Eighth War Zone, with the Suiyuan provincial government at **Shanba** 陝壩 from
this year; and the Ordos, Dongsheng 東勝 and the Ikh Juu banners, never
occupied. Both appear in Mengjiang's administrative tables. Neither is drawn.

Fu Zuoyi's raid on Baotou, 17–24 December, is driven off. Baotou remains
Japanese.

## 1940 — six weeks past Baotou, then back

The only year in which the core zone changes shape, and it changes back.

| Settlement | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Wuyuan 五原 | **Held 3 Feb – 20 Mar 1940** | Taken in the Hetao offensive of 28 Jan; retaken by Fu Zuoyi on 20 March, the Japanese special-service agency of about 34 men destroyed |
| Linhe 臨河 | **Held Feb – Mar 1940** | Taken with Wuyuan; recovered in the same operation |
| Shanba 陝壩 | Disputed | Treated here as not taken — the Eighth War Zone's deputy headquarters and the provincial government functioned there throughout. But Chinese accounts of the March counter-attack list Shanba among the places Fu Zuoyi "recovered", alongside Linhe and the two Wuyuan towns, which would mean it changed hands too. It sits just outside the drawn salient; at this scale the distinction is about ten kilometres |
| Beise Temple 貝子廟 [Xilinhot] | Likely | Shilingol League office moves here from Sunid Left Banner in June 1940 |

The Japanese main force withdrew east to its start line around 1 March, before
the counter-attack — Okabe judged permanent occupation of the Hetao impossible
with the troops available. **After 28 March 1940 the line does not move again
until the surrender.**

Everything else is as 1939.

---

## 1941 — the line stops moving

No territorial change. There is no further Japanese offensive of any size in
Suiyuan after March 1940, and the western Suiyuan garrison mounts no
large-scale attacks for the rest of the war.

The fighting moves inside the zone: five or six attacks on the Eighth Route
Army's organs in western Suiyuan and more than ten sweeps across western Inner
Mongolia between May and October. On 31 August Mengjiang is renamed the Mongol
Autonomous State for internal purposes.

Settlement list as 1940 minus the Hetao.

---

## 1942 — no territorial change

Sweeps of the Suinan guerrilla zone with 1,000 and then 2,000 troops in
January–February; a wider campaign built on a "general war" doctrine and the
"administrative leapfrog" movement. The Daqingshan contested zone contracts
sharply but is not eliminated.

The 4th Cavalry Brigade leaves the Garrison Army in December and the remaining
cavalry becomes the 3rd Tank Division — the garrison is being thinned, not
reinforced.

Settlement list unchanged.

---

## 1943 — no territorial change

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| Xuanhua 宣化 | The South Chahar administrative office becomes **Xuanhua province** on 1 Jan 1943 |
| Datong 大同 | The North Shanxi office becomes **Datong province** the same day |

The Ikh Juu League Incident of February–April is a revolt of Mongol banner
troops against **Nationalist** rule in the Ordos. Japan tried to exploit it and
gained no ground: the Ordos stays outside the occupied zone.

1943 is the worst year in the Daqingshan — fighters frequently without food,
the base surviving by dispersal — but no county town changes hands.

Settlement list unchanged.

---

## 1944 — no territorial change

Nothing in this theatre moves. The Ichigō offensive is fought a thousand
kilometres to the south.

Settlement list unchanged.

## 1945 — collapse from the north in twelve days

The position on 10 August is exactly the position of 28 March 1940. Then:

| Date | Settlement | Lost to |
|---|---|---|
| 11 Aug | Border crossed; drive on Dolonnor and Zhangjiakou | Soviet–Mongolian forces |
| 14–16 Aug | Huade 化德, Shangdu 商都 | Soviet–Mongolian forces |
| 16 Aug | Zhangbei 張北 | Soviet–Mongolian forces |
| 19 Aug | — | Mengjiang government dissolves; Demchugdongrub flees to Peiping |
| 20–21 Aug | Langwogou 狼窩溝 line | Soviet–Mongolian forces |
| 22 Aug | Jining 集寧, Fengzhen 豐鎮 | Eighth Route Army (Jin–Cha–Ji) |
| 23 Aug | Zhangjiakou 張家口 | Eighth Route Army (Pingbei sub-district) |
| 23 Aug | Bailingmiao 百霊廟, Dolonnor 多倫, Shangdu | Eighth Route Army and Suiyuan–Mongolia units meeting Soviet–Mongolian forces |
| by 27 Aug | Everything south of the Great Wall evacuated | Mongolia Garrison Army withdraws after a delaying action to 21 Aug covering ~40,000 civilians |

**Guisui and Baotou** are the exception. They are not taken in August; they pass
to Fu Zuoyi, who was named surrender-receiving officer for Rehe, Chahar and
Suiyuan on 18 August, and Communist forces fail to take either of them in the
Pingsui railway campaign of 18 October – 14 December.

The map for 1945 shows the position on the eve of 11 August, which is the
position the occupation held for its last five years.

## How the polygons were drawn

The zone in `suiyuan-history.html` is not freehand. It is Suiyuan and Chahar
provinces entire, plus the North Shanxi counties, unioned into a single polygon
and then cut. The provincial rings come from the `1928-45` layer of the Modern
East Asia GIS *China Provincial Boundaries* set, already in WGS84. Wherever an
edge is a real boundary it *is* the real boundary, vertex for vertex: the whole
northern side is the exact border with Outer Mongolia, and the eastern edge
against Manchukuo, the Chahar–Hebei line, and the Suiyuan–Shanxi line down the
Yellow River are all as surveyed. The same rings, untouched, are what the
province overlay draws.

Four cuts are made against that union. Only these are drawn rather than
surveyed.

1. **The inner Great Wall** — Ningwu, Yanmen, Pingxing — as the southern limit
   of the North Shanxi counties. This is what the *Mengjiang Regional Map*
   itself follows along the south, and it is why the zone dips below Datong and
   notches back up around Shuozhou. Kept angular: it is a real line on the
   ground, and rounding it would be a falsification. Its eastern end stops at
   the limit of Lingqiu county rather than running on to Zijingguan, because
   the boundary file's Shanxi polygon reaches over Laiyuan, which was Hebei.
2. **The Yellow River**, traced from Baotou east to the Hekou bend at Togtoh,
   where the river turns south and the Suiyuan–Shanxi provincial boundary takes
   over. The trace runs slightly north of the true bank, which correctly leaves
   the thin north-bank strip of Dalad and Jungar banners inside the zone — the
   part of the Ordos Mengjiang did administer.
3. **A rounded cap west of Baotou.** Control ended a short way past the
   railhead. Nothing here is a boundary of any kind, so it is drawn as a curve
   rather than a ruled meridian.
4. **The eastern limit** (below), rounded for the same reason.

The **eastern edge follows the Mengjiang map rather than the boundary file.**
The file carries Chahar province well east of anything the regime administered
and throws out a lobe toward Chengde — Fengning and Luanping, which were Rehe
and so Manchukuo. That lobe does not appear on the Mengjiang map and is not
drawn here.

The rule applied throughout: what the Mengjiang map shows as inside is kept,
and no more; what it claims but the regime never held — the Hetao west of
Baotou, and the Ordos — is left out; elsewhere the shape is preserved, and
corners are rounded unless they follow an actual provincial boundary or the
Wall.

All forty-four settlements listed above as held were tested by point-in-polygon
against the zone, and all fall inside it. So were seventeen negative controls —
Shanba, Dongsheng, Uxin, Ejin Horo, Wutai, Fuping, Daixian, Ningwu, Hequ,
Baode, Fanshi, Laiyuan, Chengde, Fengning, Luanping, and Wuyuan and Linhe
outside their six weeks — and all fall outside it.

The extent follows `Mengjiang_North_Shanxi_South_Chahar_Map_c1937-1939.png`,
the regime's own *Mengjiang Regional Map*, with the internal geography checked
against `Administrative_map_of_the_United_Mongol_Autonomous_Government.png`.
Both are in this directory. What that map shows as inside is kept and no more;
what it claims but the regime never held is left out.

## Summary: the shape, in one paragraph

From 17 October 1937 to 11 August 1945 the occupied zone in these two provinces
was a corridor about 700 km long and seldom more than 100 km wide, running from
Huailai through Zhangjiakou, Datong, Jining and Guisui to Baotou, with two
lobes hanging off it — the Zhangjiakou basin and the northern Shanxi
counties — and a thin steppe administration reaching north over the Chahar,
Ulanchab and Shilingol leagues. Along its northern flank, for seven of those
eight years, ran a mountain belt it never held. West of Baotou it held nothing
except for six weeks. South of the Yellow River it held nothing at all.
