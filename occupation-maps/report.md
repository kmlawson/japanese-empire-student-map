# Chahar and Suiyuan, 1935–1945

A timeline of the Japanese advance into the two provinces, and of what was
actually held as against what was claimed. Compiled from Japanese- and
Chinese-language sources; every claim is referenced in `sources.md`.

The short version: Japan took the railway and the towns on it, and never took
anything else. From October 1937 to August 1945 the western end of the line
stopped at Baotou and did not move. Everything west of Baotou — the Hetao
irrigated plain, the Ordos loop south of the Yellow River — stayed under the
Nationalist Eighth War Zone for the whole war, apart from seven weeks in the
spring of 1940. The mountains in between, the Daqingshan, were held by nobody
and contested by the Eighth Route Army until the end.

Place names are given in the form used at the time, with the modern name on
first mention. Guisui (歸綏) is today's Hohhot; the Mengjiang regime renamed it
Kōwa/Houhe (厚和豪特) in 1937.

---

## 1. The geography that decides the whole story

Two facts govern everything below.

**The Beiping–Suiyuan railway.** A single line runs west from Peking through
Nankou, Zhangjiakou (Kalgan), Datong, Fengzhen, Jining, Guisui and ends at
Baotou. Every town Japan held in the two provinces sat on this line or within a
short march of it. The line was built to Baotou and stopped there: west of
Baotou there was no railway, and Japanese logistics west of Baotou therefore
depended on cart tracks across 200 km of desert steppe. This is why the
occupation stops where it does.

**The Yellow River and the Yin/Daqing mountains.** North of the railway the
Daqingshan (Yinshan) range runs east–west, rough country that a garrison army
cannot police and a guerrilla force can live in. South and west of Baotou the
Yellow River loops north then south around the Ordos plateau; the irrigated
Hetao ("Houtao", 後套) plain inside the northern bend around Wuyuan and Linhe
was the richest agricultural land in Suiyuan and the base from which Fu Zuoyi
fought for eight years.

## 2. Timeline

### Background: 1933–34

Japanese forces took Dolonnor (多倫) in Chahar during the Rehe campaign of 1933.
On **22 September 1933** the Kwantung Army set up the **Chahar East Special
Autonomous District** (察東特別自治区) there under the Mongol officer Li Shouxin
(李守信), modelled on Manchukuo, with about 4,600 troops and, from July 1935,
funded through the Manchukuo Army's Political Department. This is the seed of
everything that follows.

### 1935 — Chahar surrendered by agreement, not by battle

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **15 Jan 1935** | First Chahar East Incident. Japanese-officered Rehe forces attack Chinese positions at Changliang and Wunihe in Guyuan county. Wang Ziliang's troops repel them. |
| **23–25 Jan 1935** | Japanese aircraft bomb Dushikou and Guyuan; roughly 3,000 Japanese and 2,000 puppet troops attack on the ground. |
| **2 Feb 1935** | **Datan oral accord** (大灘口約), agreed at the Japanese HQ at Datan in Fengning. Chinese forces are to stay west of the Great Wall line at Shichengzi and Dongzhazi. About 700 km² east of the Guyuan–Dushikou line becomes a de facto demilitarised zone. |
| **5 Jun 1935** | Second Zhangbei Incident. Four Japanese officers travelling without passports and sketching maps are detained near Zhangbei by the 29th Army and released after eight hours. |
| **11 Jun 1935** | Second Chahar East Incident: Japanese and Manchukuo troops re-enter Dongzhazi. |
| **27 Jun 1935** | **Ching–Doihara agreement** (秦土協定). Song Zheyuan's 29th Army withdraws from Zhangbei county and everything north of it; two security forces of 4,000 men take over. In effect the whole of Chahar north of the Great Wall becomes demilitarised. Chinese accounts put the loss at 70–80% of the province's territory. |

**Control at end of 1935.** No Japanese army of occupation is in Chahar. But
Chinese regular troops have been pushed out of the northern two-thirds of the
province by treaty, leaving a vacuum that the Kwantung Army fills with Mongol
clients. Suiyuan is untouched.

### 1936 — the Mongol client state, and the first defeat

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Feb 1936** | Demchugdongrub (德王, "Teh Wang") establishes a Mongol Military Headquarters; Li Shouxin as deputy commander. |
| **12 May 1936** | **Mongol Military Government** (蒙古軍政府) proclaimed at Dehua (德化, i.e. Huade). Prince Yun (雲王) chairman, Demchugdongrub commander-in-chief, Li Shouxin chief of staff. Kwantung Army advisers — Muratani Hikojirō as chief adviser, Yamauchi as military adviser — hold the real authority. The Chahar East Special District is absorbed into it. |
| **5 Nov 1936** | Japanese planning conference at Jiabusi decides on an advance into eastern Suiyuan, aiming at Honggeertu and beyond it the provincial capital Guisui. |
| **14/15–18/19 Nov 1936** | **Honggeertu** (紅格爾図). Wang Ying's "Great Han Righteous Army" (大漢義軍) and Mongol units, 1,500–5,000 men with Japanese aircraft, artillery and armoured cars, attack. Fu Zuoyi directs the defence from Pingdiquan. The attack fails; the attackers fall back on Shangdu on the 19th. |
| **23–24 Nov 1936** | **Bailingmiao** (百霊廟). Fu Zuoyi's Jin–Sui troops storm the lamasery at night and take it by 09:30 on the 24th. The Japanese adviser and the puppet 7th Division commander escape by car. |
| **2–4 Dec 1936** | Puppet counter-attack on Bailingmiao under Lei Zhongtian is destroyed; Lei is killed. |
| **8–9 Dec 1936** | At Xilamulen Temple the puppet brigade commanders Shi Yushan and Jin Xianzhang defect with some 6,000 men, wipe out the Mukedengbao division and kill around 20–29 Japanese officers. |
| **19 Dec 1936** | Campaign ends. |

**Control at end of 1936.** Japan holds nothing in Suiyuan. The 1936 Suiyuan
campaign is the first clear Chinese battlefield victory over Japanese-sponsored
forces and was reported as such across China. Note what it was: Japanese
advisers, Japanese weapons and Japanese aircraft, but Mongol and Chinese puppet
infantry. Japan did not commit its own divisions and consequently lost nothing
it could not disown.

### 1937 — the year the map is made

The Marco Polo Bridge incident of 7 July changes the method. The Kwantung Army
had repeatedly asked the General Staff for permission to occupy Chahar and been
refused; on **8 August 1937** the Chahar Operation was approved, and it began
the next day. A **Chahar Expeditionary Force** of three brigades — colloquially
the "Tōjō force" after its commander Tōjō Hideki — drove west from Rehe, later
joined by Itagaki Seishirō's 5th Division.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **8–26 Aug 1937** | **Nankou campaign.** About 60,000 Chinese hold the pass against roughly 70,000 Japanese for eighteen days. Chinese losses given as 9,703 dead and 23,989 wounded. |
| **14 / 16 Aug 1937** | Chinese forces briefly retake Shangdu and Huade in northern Chahar. |
| **20–21 Aug 1937** | Japanese attack Juyongguan; Chinese pull back from the Zhangbei sector. |
| **23–24 Aug 1937** | Shenweitai falls; Wanquan falls the next day. |
| **27 Aug 1937** | **Zhangjiakou falls.** Chinese forces are ordered to break contact. Huailai is occupied that night. |
| **28 Aug 1937** | Xuanhua falls. |
| **13 Sep 1937** | **Datong falls.** Fengzhen and Jining are taken in September. |
| **4 Sep 1937** | **South Chahar Autonomous Government** (察南自治政府) set up at Zhangjiakou. |
| **14 Oct 1937** | **Guisui falls** — the capital of Suiyuan. |
| **15 Oct 1937** | **North Shanxi Autonomous Government** (晋北自治政府) set up at Datong. |
| **16 Oct 1937** | Salaqi taken. |
| **17 Oct 1937** | **Baotou falls.** The Chahar Operation formally ends. *Japanese control in Suiyuan will not extend further west than this at any point in the next eight years, except for six weeks in 1940.* |
| **26 Oct 1937** | Yang Chengwu's independent regiment takes Yu county (蔚縣) in southern Chahar — the first Communist foothold in the province. |
| **27 Oct / 7 Nov 1937** | Jin–Cha–Ji Military Region established (HQ formed at Wutai on 27 Oct; region formally established 7 Nov) under Nie Rongzhen, with about 3,000 men. |
| **28 Oct 1937** | **Mongol League Autonomous Government** (蒙古聯盟自治政府) proclaimed at Guisui, renamed Kōwa. |
| **22 Nov 1937** | **Mengjiang Joint Committee** (蒙疆聯合委員会) formed at Zhangjiakou to coordinate the three autonomous governments; the Mengjiang Bank is set up the same day. The term "Mengjiang" as a regional unit dates from here. |

Japanese accounts are candid that the advance outran its logistics: supply
failed to keep pace and units in the follow-on echelons went hungry.

### 1938 — the occupation regime, and the Communist counter-move

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **4 Jan 1938** | **Mongolia Garrison Detachment** (駐蒙兵団) formed by Continental Order No. 42, directly under Imperial General Headquarters, responsible for security in Inner Mongolia, southern Chahar and northern Shanxi. |
| **10 Jan 1938** | Jin–Cha–Ji Border Region Administrative Committee elected at Fuping — the first Communist-led base-area government behind Japanese lines. |
| **4 Jul 1938** | Detachment reorganised as the **Mongolia Garrison Army** (駐蒙軍) and placed under the North China Area Army. |
| **Late Jun 1938** | The 120th Division forms the **Daqingshan Detachment** around the 715th Regiment, under Li Jingquan. |
| **29 Jul 1938** | The Daqingshan Detachment, the 4th Guerrilla Detachment and a mobilisation-committee cadre group — about 2,300 men — set out for the Daqingshan. |
| **Sep 1938** | Wugongba ambush: 80-odd Japanese killed in 25 minutes. |
| **Nov 1938** | The detachment converts from infantry to cavalry to survive in the steppe. |
| **By Dec 1938** | Three guerrilla zones established — Suizhong, Suixi, Suinan — around and through the Daqingshan, astride the railway Japan depends on. |

### 1939 — consolidation, and the first probe west

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Jan 1939** | A Cavalry Group (1st and 4th Cavalry Brigades) is added to the Mongolia Garrison Army. |
| **1 Sep 1939** | The three autonomous governments merge into the **Mengjiang United Autonomous Government** (蒙古聯合自治政府), capital Zhangjiakou, Demchugdongrub chairman. Structure: five leagues (Bayantala, Chahar, Shilingol, Ulanchab, Ikh Juu), two administrative offices (South Chahar, North Shanxi) and two special cities (Kōwa, Baotou). Japanese supreme advisers hold the actual decision-making power and much of the government's paperwork is produced in Japanese. |
| **1939** | The Nationalist Suiyuan provincial government relocates to **Shanba** (陝壩) in the Hetao, which becomes the wartime provincial capital. Fu Zuoyi is both provincial chairman and deputy commander of the Eighth War Zone. |
| **17–24 Dec 1939** | **Baotou raid.** Fu Zuoyi's 35th Army attacks Baotou, where the Japanese cavalry command is based, in the winter offensive. He is driven off by counter-attack but demonstrates that the westernmost Japanese garrison is exposed. |

### 1940 — the seven weeks Japan held the Hetao

This is the only moment in the whole war when Japanese control in Suiyuan
extends past Baotou.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **28 Jan 1940** | The Mongolia Garrison Army under Okabe Naozaburō launches the **Hetao offensive** (後套進攻作戦 / 五原作戦), three columns west along the Yellow River. Forces given as the 26th Division, the Cavalry Group and the 2nd Independent Mixed Brigade — some 30,000 men — plus three divisions each of Mongol and West Suiyuan puppet troops. |
| **1–2 Feb 1940** | Chinese positions broken. |
| **3 Feb 1940** | **Wuyuan occupied**; Linhe also taken. |
| **~1 Mar 1940** | Japanese main force withdraws east to its start line, leaving a puppet garrison and a special-service agency behind. This was deliberate: the Garrison Army's chief of staff wanted permanent occupation of the Hetao, but Okabe judged it impossible with the troops available and defined the operation's object as destruction of Fu's field army, not the taking of ground. |
| **20–22 Mar 1940** | **Recapture of Wuyuan.** Assault parties from the 35th Army's 31st Division (An Chunshan), 32nd Division (Yuan Qingrong) and 101st Division (Dong Qiwu) infiltrate the new and old towns at midnight on the 20th. The Japanese special-service agency — around 34 men — is wiped out. |
| **28 Mar 1940** | Operations end. Chinese forces hold Wuyuan, Linhe and Shanba. |

Chinese propaganda called this the "Wuyuan great victory" and it was
publicised as the first campaign of the war in which Chinese forces retook lost
territory and kept it. That claim is essentially true for this theatre: the
line established in late March 1940 did not move again until the Japanese
surrender.

### 1941–1944 — a static front and a war against the base areas

There is no further Japanese offensive of any size in Suiyuan after March 1940.
Japanese sources record that from then on the garrison in western Suiyuan
mounted no large-scale attacks. The fighting moves entirely to the interior of
the occupied zone.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Jun 1940** | Shilingol League office moves from Sunid Left Banner to Beise Temple (貝子廟) — a useful dating marker for Mengjiang's own maps. |
| **31 Aug 1941** | Mengjiang renamed the **Mongol Autonomous State** (蒙古自治邦) for internal purposes, at Demchugdongrub's urging and with Japanese army backing; the old name is kept for external use. |
| **May–Oct 1941** | Japanese and puppet forces attack the Eighth Route Army's party and government organs in western Suiyuan five or six times and mount more than ten sweeps across western Inner Mongolia. |
| **Jan–Feb 1942** | Sweeps of the Suinan guerrilla zone with 1,000 and then 2,000 troops. |
| **1942** | Major sweeps of the Daqingshan base under a "general war" doctrine built around the "administrative leapfrog" campaign. |
| **24 Oct 1942** | The Daqingshan Cavalry Detachment is merged with forces from northern Shanxi to form the **Saibei Military Sub-district** under Yao Zhe. |
| **Dec 1942** | The 4th Cavalry Brigade leaves the Garrison Army for the 12th Army; the remaining cavalry is converted into the **3rd Tank Division**. |
| **1 Jan 1943** | The South Chahar administrative office becomes **Xuanhua province** and the North Shanxi office becomes **Datong province** — the last significant change to Mengjiang's internal structure. |
| **21 Feb – Apr 1943** | **Ikh Juu League Incident** (伊盟事變) in the Ordos. This is a revolt of Mongol banner troops against *Nationalist* rule, not a Japanese advance: on 26 March some 400 Zasak Banner security troops attack the Nationalist party headquarters and the Sui–Mongol political council, and on 15 April over 2,000 Ushin Banner troops and civilians rise as well. Chen Changjie and He Wending suppress it with the Ikh Juu defence cavalry. Mao Zedong later cited it in *On Coalition Government* as an error of Han chauvinism. Japan attempted to exploit the revolt but did not gain control of the Ordos. |
| **1943** | The hardest year for the Daqingshan base — fighters frequently without food, widespread malnutrition. Yao Zhe's response is a policy of concealed struggle: preserve the force, do not seek battle. |
| **1944** | No significant change to the line. |

### 1945 — collapse from the north

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **Jul 1945** | The Saibei Military Sub-district is upgraded to the **Suiyuan–Mongolia Military Region** (綏蒙軍区) under Yao Zhe. |
| **8 / 10 Aug 1945** | The USSR declares war on Japan; the Mongolian People's Republic follows on the 10th. |
| **11 Aug 1945** | Soviet–Mongolian cavalry-mechanised forces cross from Outer Mongolia into Inner Mongolia, driving on Dolonnor and Zhangjiakou and cutting the link between the Kwantung Army and the China Expeditionary Army. |
| **~14–16 Aug 1945** | Huade and Shangdu taken; Soviet–Mongolian troops enter Zhangbei on the 16th. |
| **15 Aug 1945** | Guo Tianmin, commanding the Ji–Cha Military Region, orders Zhan Danan and Duan Suquan to take Zhangjiakou with the Pingbei sub-district troops. |
| **18 Aug 1945** | Chiang Kai-shek names Fu Zuoyi the officer to receive the Japanese surrender in Rehe, Chahar and Suiyuan — a paper claim to territory Communist troops were already entering. |
| **19 Aug 1945** | The Mengjiang government collapses. Demchugdongrub and Li Shouxin flee to Peiping with the withdrawing Garrison Army headquarters. |
| **20–21 Aug 1945** | Soviet–Mongolian forces take the whole Japanese position at Langwogou. |
| **21 Aug 1945** | The Mongolia Garrison Army, under Nemoto Hiroshi with Nakagawa Tomao as chief of staff, fights a delaying action until this date to complete the evacuation of about 40,000 Japanese civilians. It had been ordered to surrender to Soviet forces and judged the order unrealistic. |
| **22 Aug 1945** | Eighth Route Army Jin–Cha–Ji units take Jining and Fengzhen, the strategic points on the railway. |
| **23 Aug 1945** | Pingbei sub-district troops occupy **Zhangjiakou** in the morning. Eighth Route Army and Suiyuan–Mongolia units link up with Soviet–Mongolian forces at Shangdu, Dolonnor and Bailingmiao. |
| **27 Aug 1945** | The Mongolia Garrison Army completes its withdrawal south of the Great Wall. It is formally demobilised on 27 July 1946. |
| **18 Oct – 14 Dec 1945** | The **Pingsui railway campaign** — Communist forces under Nie Rongzhen and He Long (about 53,000) against Fu Zuoyi (about 60,000). The Communists take six eastern Suiyuan counties but fail to take Guisui or Baotou and withdraw. The occupation ends; the civil war for the same railway begins immediately. |

## 3. Who actually held what

### Held continuously by Japan and its clients, Oct 1937 – Aug 1945

The **Pingsui railway corridor** and the county towns on or beside it:
Zhangjiakou, Xuanhua, Wanquan, Huailai; Datong, Fengzhen, Jining; Guisui/Kōwa,
Salaqi, Baotou. In Suiyuan this amounts to the Bayantala League — Guisui,
Salaqi, Qingshuihe, Togtoh, Horinger, Guyang, Wuchuan, Fengzhen, Xinghe,
Taolin, Jining and Liangcheng counties — administered as the core of Mengjiang.

**Northern Chahar** — Zhangbei, Shangdu, Kangbao, Baochang, Guyuan, Dolonnor —
had already been evacuated by Chinese regulars under the 1935 agreements and
passed to Mongol administration; after 1937 it formed the Chahar League with
its office at Zhangbei.

The steppe leagues, **Shilingol** (office at Sunid Left Banner, from June 1940
at Beise Temple) and **Ulanchab** (office at Bailingmiao), were held in the
sense that the banner administrations were run through Mengjiang and Japanese
advisers. Actual Japanese presence was thin: garrison posts, not occupation.

### Never held by Japan

**The Hetao / Houtao plain** west of Baotou — Wuyuan, Linhe, Anbei, Micang,
Langshan, Yanjiang counties and the wartime provincial capital Shanba — was
Nationalist throughout, except **3 February to 20 March 1940**. This was not a
guerrilla zone but a functioning province: Fu Zuoyi ran a civil administration,
irrigation works and land reclamation there for the entire war.

**The Ordos** (Ikh Juu League) south of the Yellow River was never occupied.
It remained under Nationalist authority, contested internally by the 1943
banner revolt but not by Japanese troops.

### Contested throughout — the resistance zones inside the line

**The Daqingshan base** (大青山抗日游擊根據地), from July 1938 to 1945. Three
guerrilla zones — Suizhong, Suixi, Suinan — in the mountains immediately north
of the Guisui–Baotou railway. This force never held a county town. It held
rural districts and mountain valleys, and it sat directly on top of the railway
that the Japanese position in Suiyuan depended on, which is why it drew ten-plus
sweeps in 1941 alone. It was reduced almost to extinction in 1942–43 and
survived by dispersal, expanding again only in 1945.

**The Jin–Cha–Ji border region**, from November 1937. Southern Chahar and the
Yanbei district of northern Shanxi — inside the South Chahar and North Shanxi
puppet governments' nominal territory — carried a Communist base-area
administration from January 1938 onward.

So the shading on any map of this region has to be read as three layers: towns
and railway under real Japanese administration; steppe banners under
Mongol administration with Japanese advisers and little garrison; and mountain
and rural districts inside both where Japanese authority existed only when a
column was physically present.

### The Manchukuo question

Manchukuo never annexed any part of Chahar or Suiyuan. Its involvement was
indirect and confined to 1933–36: the Chahar East Special Autonomous District
at Dolonnor was Kwantung Army-run and Manchukuo-funded from July 1935, and
Manchukuo forces took part in the Chahar East incidents of January and June
1935. From May 1936 that territory passed to Demchugdongrub's Mongol Military
Government and from 1937 into Mengjiang. After 1937 the Manchukuo–Mengjiang
boundary followed the old Rehe–Chahar provincial line.

## 4. What the regime's own maps admit

The strongest single piece of evidence for the gap between claimed and actual
control comes from the Mengjiang government's own cartography, analysed by
Futaki Hiroshi (二木博史) of the Japan–Mongolia Society in 2021.

Five administrative-division maps printed in Japanese by the regime are known,
held at Tokyo University of Foreign Studies, the Institute of Developing
Economies, Kyoto University's Institute for Research in Humanities and
Hitotsubashi. On the Publicity Section's undated `蒙疆行政区画地図`, two banners
that belong administratively to the Ikh Juu League — Dalad and Jungar, the
Ordos left-wing rear and front banners — are drawn *inside* Bayantala League,
even though the map's own table of administrative divisions lists them under
Ikh Juu. Urad Front Banner and Anbei county are moved the same way.

Futaki's reading is that only the northern strips of those banners, along the
Yellow River, were under effective Mengjiang control, while most of the Ordos
was in fact administered by the Nationalist government — and that the map's
draughtsmen adopted a rule of drawing whatever the regime actually controlled
inside Bayantala. Which means that on the regime's own map, **"Ikh Juu League"
functions as a label for the territory Mengjiang did not control.**

The corroboration is that the Land Survey Department in Tokyo, drawing the
`二百万分一蒙疆素図` in April 1942, drew Bayantala, Ulanchab and Ikh Juu on their
proper boundaries. Tokyo mapped the claim; Zhangjiakou mapped the reality.

A second admission of the same kind: the Garrison Army headquarters' own
military terrain-intelligence maps, `内蒙古地方五十万分一地誌図` of April 1939
(classified), do not use Mengjiang's administrative divisions at all. They keep
the Republic of China's "Suiyuan province".

Two of the published maps also get the Hetao wrong in a revealing direction.
The `蒙疆最新全図` supplements to the *Mengjiang Shimbun* place Wuyuan and Linhe
counties in Ulanchab League rather than Ikh Juu — assigning to a controlled
league two counties that were in Nationalist hands when the map was printed.

## 5. Disputed and uncertain points

These are places where the sources do not agree and the report above should not
be read as settled.

**Nankou casualty figures.** The figure of about 15,000 Japanese casualties is
from Chinese accounts and is not corroborated on the Japanese side. The Chinese
figures for their own losses (9,703 dead, 23,989 wounded) are more likely to be
reliable.

**Mizukawa Isao (水川伊夫).** Chinese sources uniformly state that a Japanese
lieutenant general of this name commanding the West Suiyuan garrison was killed
outside Wuyuan on 22 March 1940, and he is regularly listed among the general
officers killed in China. Japanese biographical records instead show a Mizukawa
Isao who held police and public-security posts in the Mengjiang government
between 1933 and September 1939 — not an army officer at all, let alone a
lieutenant general — and a man of that name was given a funeral in Okayama some
45 years later. Someone senior was killed at Wuyuan; the rank and identity as
usually given in Chinese accounts do not survive scrutiny.

**Wuyuan casualty claims.** The Chinese figures — 300-plus Japanese killed, the
entire special-service agency destroyed, 50-plus prisoners, two puppet divisions
of Wang Ying's force annihilated — come from Nationalist reporting of a victory
that was politically important at the time. The destruction of the
special-service detachment (about 34 men) is well attested; the wider figures
are not independently confirmed.

**Mengjiang's population.** Official statistics give 6,419,374; the figure
usually treated as the real one is 5,747,374. Either way the population was
overwhelmingly Han Chinese, not Mongol — which is the central contradiction of
a state built on Mongol autonomy. Demchugdongrub's nationalists regarded the
merger with the Han-majority South Chahar and North Shanxi governments in 1937
as a defeat, and the 1939 unified government as a step toward becoming "a second
Manchukuo".

**Dates for Fengzhen and Jining, 1937.** Chinese sources say September without a
day; the railway sequence makes mid-to-late September near-certain but the
report gives no precise date because none is well attested.

## 6. The economic layer

Worth noting because it explains why Japan wanted the corridor and not the
countryside. The Mengjiang Bank was established with the Joint Committee on 22
November 1937. Opium purchasing was run through a single monopoly organ under
the autonomous government with revenue flowing to the Kwantung Army. The iron
ore at Longyan near Xuanhua was worked under Japanese military direction, with
reserves put at 91.645 million tonnes by 1941, alongside regional coal. Wool
from the steppe leagues was a controlled commodity. All of this is railway
freight: it needs Zhangjiakou–Datong–Baotou and it does not need the Hetao.

## 8. What this means for the occupied-zone shading

Three corrections follow for `OCCUPIED_ZONE` in `tools/build_map.py`, if the
line is ever drawn at a scale where this region is legible:

1. **The western terminus is Baotou, not the Suiyuan provincial boundary.**
   From 17 October 1937 to August 1945 the occupied zone ends there. Wuyuan and
   Linhe were held for six weeks in 1940 and are outside the line for any date
   other than February–March 1940.
2. **The Ordos is outside the line entirely.** The Yellow River's northern bend
   is the boundary; nothing south of it in the Ikh Juu League was occupied.
3. **North of the railway the line should be a corridor, not a province.** The
   Daqingshan was guerrilla country from 1938 onward, and the steppe leagues
   north of it were administered rather than garrisoned. If the map elsewhere
   treats "where Japanese authority reached" as the criterion — the caveat
   already in this directory's README — then the steppe belongs inside the line
   and the Daqingshan is the same kind of exception as Jin–Cha–Ji further east.
